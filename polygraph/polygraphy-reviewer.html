Foundations of lie detection

Key definitions and the stimulus-response theory behind the polygraph.

Lie detection and psychophysiology

Lie detection customarily means recording a person’s psycho-physiological reactions while questioned, then having trained experts interpret them. Scientific methods combine psychology and physiology, so the field is called psychophysiology: the study of the nervous system and how bodily reactions are influenced by mental responses to a specific stimulus. Examiners must therefore know the human nervous system.

What is polygraphy?

DefinitionPolygraphy is the scientific detection of deception through the use or aid of a polygraph.

Polygraph comes from Greek poly (many) and graphos (writings). It is an instrument that records changes in blood pressure/pulse, respiration, and galvanic skin reflex (GSR) as indicators of emotional excitement, especially lying when questioned. The polygraph is one of the scientific instruments of crime detection in police departments.

Important terms

Deception
Act of deceiving or misleading, accomplished by lying.
Detection
Discovering the existence of something hidden or obscured.
Lying
Creating a false or misleading impression with intent to wrongfully affect another’s acts, opinion, or affection. Done verbally, or by acts, feigns, ruses, marks and symbols.
Emotion
Acute disturbance of the individual as a whole, psychological in origin, involving behavior, conscious experience, and visceral functions.
Fear
Emotional response to a specific danger that seems beyond one’s defensive power.
Heredity
Transmission of mental and physical traits from parents to offspring.
Lie detector
The “man”: the investigator or polygraph examiner who determines deception, not the machine.
Normal tracing
Tracing produced when an irrelevant question is asked.
Dicrotic notch
Short horizontal line on the cardio tracing at the middle of the diastolic stem.
Chart / polygram
Composite record of the pneumograph, galvanograph, and cardiosphygmograph tracings from one series of questions.
Circumstantial evidence
Series of events from which guilt or innocence is inferred.
Subject
Any person undergoing examination: suspect, complainant, witness, victim, relative, or job applicant (loyalty or integrity screening).
Examiner / polygraphist
The expert conducting the test.

Stimulus-response theory

Stimulus→Reaction→Response→Specific response
  • Stimulus: force from the environment that excites receptors (a question that acts as a stressor).
  • Reaction: mental (psychological) activity aroused by the stimulus.
  • Response: muscular or glandular (physiological) change, e.g., faster breathing and heartbeat.
  • Specific response: deviation from the norm shown to a particular question (sweating, nervousness).

Objective, accuracy, and uses

  • Principle: a conscious mental effort to deceive produces involuntary physiological changes (respiration, blood pressure/pulse, GSR). Changes from an established baseline are presumed to indicate emotional reaction when lying.
  • Accuracy depends on the competency of the examiner.
  • Objectives: obtain an admission (self-incriminating statement short of acknowledging guilt) or confession (voluntary acknowledgment of guilt with circumstances).
  • Questions are answerable by yes or no; the examiner interprets observable symptoms.

Used in investigation to…

Eliminate innocent subjects, recognize fabricated complaints, determine veracity of information, narrow the inquiry, locate fruits of the crime, gather information from suspects and reluctant witnesses, speed up the investigation.

Other uses

Criminal investigation, pre-employment screening, periodic integrity checks, claim verification (insurance), loyalty tests, promotion.

LimitationOnly a willing subject may be tested, and the result is not accepted as evidence in court because interpretation may vary.

Methods of detecting deception

From superstition to science.

Early methods: trial by ordeal

Ordeal (from medieval Latin dei iudicium, “miraculous decisions”): an ancient trial in which the accused faced physical danger believed harmless if innocent. It relied on religious and superstitious belief, not psychology, and lasted from ancient times through the Middle Ages to the late 16th century.

Deception detection methods

Gathering information from the subject about the crime using a machine or device, drugs or substances, and other accessory means.

Observation methods

The investigator must be a keen observer and good psychologist. Stress from sympathetic nervous system stimulation may show as clues of deception, read through facial expressions, postural reactions, and cause-effect (stimulus-reaction) tests.

Signs of emotional tension

Color change, sweating, avoiding eye contact, changed heart rate and pulse, holding the breath, dry mouth and throat, unusual expression or behavior.

Internal guilt response

Quickening heart palpitation, gripping of the intestine, dry mouth, sinking feeling in the stomach, urge to swallow saliva.

External guilt response

Restless hands and feet, cannot look the examiner in the eye, peculiar monotone voice, fidgeting or drumming, excessive Adam’s apple movement in males.

Facial expression

Dilated eyes, protruding eyeballs, raised upper eyelid; blushing, paling, forehead sweat; lip twitching; excessive winking; throbbing temple vein.

Regular police methods

  • 5W1H (who, what, where, when, why, how); “why” is often asked five times to reach the core.
  • 3 I’s of investigation: Information gathering, Interview/Interrogation, Instrumentation (criminalistics or police science: medico-legal, forensic chemistry and toxicology, forensic photography, ballistics, questioned documents, dactyloscopy, polygraphy).

Truth serum and narco-analysis

  • Introduced by Dr. Edward Mandel House (1858 to 1938), adviser to President Woodrow Wilson.
  • “Truth serum” is a misnomer; it is a drug, e.g., hyoscine hydrobromide given in repeated doses until delirium. The subject may forget the alibi.
  • Narco-analysis: same idea with sodium amytal or sodium pentothal; depresses the brain’s inhibitory mechanism.
  • Drugs: hyoscine hydrobromide, sodium amytal, pentothal, seconal and other barbiturates, scopolamine, droperidol, trichloroethylene (inhaled anesthetic).
Why not advisableDangerous (can kill or damage the brain); unreliable; removes the subject’s free will; information not admissible in court (though useful for leads). Few doctors agree; a proper interrogation equals or beats it.

Intoxication

Alcohol “inhibits the inhibitor” (In vino veritas). The subject drinks in divided amounts until inhibitions release. Not admissible, unless the subject recalls the statement when confronted later.

Stimulus association test

Stimulus words, objects, or persons are presented and the subject answers as fast as possible. The time interval between each relevant stimulus and answer, and the reaction, are noted and studied.

Hypnotism

  • Introduced in 1778 by Franz Anton Mesmer, Austrian physician who believed in “animal magnetism.”
  • Needs the subject’s conscious cooperation; uses recovered memory through dissociation and hyper-amnesia.
  • Information is not admissible, since the subject is under the hypnotist’s influence. Strong-willed people (lawyers, accountants, physicians) are usually non-hypnotizable.

Word association test

  • Devised by Sir Francis Galton (1879); developed by Dr. Carl Gustav Jung, Swiss psychiatrist, founder of analytical psychology.
  • Stimulus words mixed with irrelevant ones; subject gives the first word that comes to mind. The key factor is the time of response, not whether the answer is yes or no.

Devices that record psycho-physiological response

Polygraph technique

Bodily functions are influenced by mental state. Lying is an emotional experience (fear or anxiety) producing automatic, self-regulating changes beyond conscious control, mobilized for survival.

Psychological stress evaluator (PSE)

Detects and graphs inaudible voice modulations from muscle micro-tremor at 8 to 14 cycles per second.

Psychology of lying

Why people lie, what kinds of lies and liars there are.

Psychological and physiological aspects

The mind influences all bodily functions. Detection rests on the idea that involuntary and automatic bodily changes accompany deception, caused not by consciousness of guilt but by fear of exposure and detection. A lying person fears detection and punishment; the resulting internal and external changes are beyond his control, and the polygraph records them.

Reasons people lie

  • Defense mechanism to avoid trouble with the law
  • Not to hurt someone’s feelings
  • They gain from it
  • To avoid embarrassment
  • For a joke

The polygraph instrument

Four major components and where each attaches.

ComponentMeasuresPlacement / pen
PneumographThoracic and abdominal breathingTubes around chest and abdomen; two top pens
GalvanographSkin resistance (GSR / electrodermal)Electrodes on index and ring finger of the left hand; third pen (longest)
CardiosphygmographBlood pressure and pulseCuff over brachial artery of the right arm; fourth (bottom) pen
KymographChart paper feedChart-recording unit

1. Pneumograph

Rubberized corrugated tube (about 7 to 10 inches) fastened around chest and abdomen; breathing expands and contracts it, moving bellows and pen. A beaded chain locks the tube. The recording unit has brass bellows, frame, crankshaft, countershaft, adjustable fulcrum, pen cradle, and pen centering control. Contributors: Benussi (1914) and Burtt (1918).

2. Cardiosphygmograph

  • Cuff with rubber bladder, inflated by hand pump to about 60 to 80 mm Hg.
  • Parts: blood pressure cuff, sphygmomanometer, pump bulb assembly, resonance control unit, centering control, vent, 5-inch recording pen.
  • Systolic: upward pressure, heart contracts. Diastolic: downward, heart relaxed. Dicrotic notch: short notch at middle of diastolic stem.
  • Pioneers: Lombroso, Mosso, Marston, Larson.

3. Galvanograph

  • Passes an imperceptible current through the fingers; the galvanometer records variation in flow.
  • Parts: finger electrode assembly (left index and ring fingers), 7-inch recording pen, amplifier unit.
  • Pioneers: Sticker (1897), Veraguth (1907), Arthur.

4. Kymograph

  • Synchronous motor drives chart at 6 to 12 inches per minute; uniform speed is vital. Vertical lines mark 5 or 10 second intervals (heavy lines every 5 seconds).
  • Pulse rate = beats counted in one division × 12.
  • Parts: chart (rolled graph paper, 12 divisions per 60 seconds), rubber roller, paper rail guide, cutter bar, pen table, synchronous motor.
  • Developed by Keeler; Mackenzie’s “Ink Polygraph.”
RememberThe last instrument attached is the first detached. The exam lasts about 5 to 8 minutes.

Models shown: Lafayette conventional 5-pen, Stoelting conventional 4-pen, LX 4000 Lafayette computerized, and handheld polygraph.

Examination procedure

Requests, four phases, subject preparation, and the examination room.

Theory, rule, and purpose

Theory

When a mentally normal person lies, physiological changes occur that the instrument records and the examiner evaluates.

Cardinal rule

Always objectivity over subjectivity.

Purpose

Determine truth or deception from emotional disturbance on questions relative to the case.

Ultimate objective

Obtain the subject’s admission or confession.

Requests and requirements (SOP)

  • Based on a formal written request from PNP, AFP, other government or private agencies, a court order, or a private individual approved by the Director, PNPCL.
  • Hand-carried by the investigator-on-case with: investigation report, sworn statements of subjects/suspects/witnesses, crime scene sketch, background and criminal records.
  • Telephone request only in emergencies pending written request; radio requests state case, number of subjects, location.
  • Covers offenses under the Revised Penal Code, Articles of War, or special laws. Civil cases only with a court or higher headquarters order.
  • A case already examined by another agency is not re-examined unless directed by the court or higher headquarters.

Four phases of the examination

1 Initial interview (investigator)→2 Pre-test interview (subject)→3 Instrumental test→4 Post-test interrogation
1. Initial interview
With the investigator: get and evaluate case facts, decide areas to ask about; basis of the questions.
2. Pre-test interview
With the subject, 20 to 30 minutes. Inform of constitutional rights (remain silent, informed of accusation, counsel, against self-incrimination); obtain consent (signed); personal data; suitability; clinical observation; psychological preparation; inform of involvement.
3. Instrumental test
Attach transducers, give instructions, stimulate and condition the subject, preliminary evaluation.
4. Post-test interrogation
If deception indicated: short interrogation for admission or confession. If truthful: release cordially and thank the subject.

ConditionRequirement

Reschedule or postpone if the subject has under 5 hours of sleep or a temporary illness (headache, monthly period, stomachache) or discomfort that may cause abnormal response.

The examination room

Quiet, private, semi-sound-proof; no distracting noises or investigators present. Conservative walls and furniture with no ornaments or pictures; well ventilated with even temperature; an adjoining observation room with one-way mirror.

Tracing patterns

Respiratory block
Appears at end of exhalation (blocking) or end of inhalation (hold).
Suppression
Nearly a block; staircase suppression is an ascending suppress cycle.
Rise from baseline
Baseline arousal.
Galvanograph deception responses
Double saddle response, rise from baseline, vertical rise point, long duration, plunging of the tracing.
Galvo level
Falls with dehydrated corneum; rises with hydrated corneum (sweat column in the duct).
Cardio deception responses
Slow pulse; roller-coaster shape.
Cardio non-deception
Consistently irregular (ectopic); relatively small (obese subject); fluctuating level (nervousness); two dicrotic notches.
Improper cuff pressure
Too low shows bulging amplitude; lower the cuff pressure to reduce it.

Rules of chart interpretation

  1. There must be a specific response.
  2. To be specific, it must deviate from the norm.
  3. It must appear in at least two test charts.
  4. Best indication of deception: specific response simultaneously in all three tracings.
Legal ruleThere must be specific responses, appearing consistently in the same relevant questions in two or more charts.

Questions and types of tests

How to formulate questions and which test to use.

Rules for question formulation

  • Simple, direct, clear, in language the subject understands.
  • No legal terms (rape, assault, murder, killed).
  • Answerable by yes or no only.
  • Not accusatory; no inferences or opinions; no reference to religion, race, or belief.
  • Each question refers to one offense only.

Types of questions

QuestionPurpose / example
IrrelevantNo relation to the case; sets the subject’s norm and lowers excitement (names, dates, position)
RelevantKey questions on the matter; strong (primary, elicits change in all three tracings) vs. weak (secondary, guilty knowledge, partial involvement)
Sacrifice / DYATAbsorbs the response to the first relevant question: “Regarding the stolen wristwatch, do you intend to answer truthfully?”
Knowledge / DYKDoes the subject know who did it or where the evidence is?
Evidence connectingLinks subject to evidence: “Were the footprints outside the house yours?”
Control (probable lie)Answerable by NO; draws a response from the innocent. Primary: based on a known lie, within 3 to 5 years before the case (“Before age 20, have you ever stolen anything?”). Secondary: more specific, up to the present, unrelated to the case
SymptomaticDetects outside-issue concern: “Are you afraid I will ask something I said I would not?”
SKY (Backster)Suspect, Know, You: confirms previous charts; Y equals the strong relevant

Types of tests

If deception is indicated, at least five tests are given.

TestDescription
General question test (GQT)Planned series of irrelevant, relevant, and control questions contrasting responses; gets a standard tracing and truth-telling pattern

What happens on the chart

The question is a verbal stimulus; the brain evaluates it. If it is no threat, the body continues normally. If it matters to the subject, the hypothalamus (control center for emotional expression) triggers a fight-or-flight response: higher pulse and blood pressure, recorded by the instrument.

Admissibility, accuracy, and Philippine cases

What a polygraph result is worth in court.

Is the result conclusive?

AnswerNo. A polygraph result is opinionated (the examiner’s interpretation).

Admissibility

  • Not accepted as court evidence in general; may be admitted by stipulation or court order.
  • Another route (per the slides): the defense shows the examiner tested the accused at the investigator’s request and offers the technical report; the court may accept it on the presumption of suppressed evidence, since the finding is against the investigator’s finding.
  • In the U.S., almost all courts use polygraph and over half accept it as evidence in criminal prosecution.

Factors affecting accuracy

The instrument, the condition of the subject, the condition of the examination room, and the qualification and skill of the examiner.

Accuracy

With a properly trained examiner using established procedures, decisions are generally in the range of 85 to 95% for specific-issue investigations (slide figure).

  • Civil: Civil Case 58092 (Isaguirre), sum of money, RTC Pasig Br. 162; Civil Case 91-3453 (Reyes v. BPI), RTC Makati Br. 142.

Interrogation tactics

Used in the post-test phase.

When guilt is definite or reasonably certain

  • Display an air of confidence in the subject’s guilt.
  • Point out some circumstantial evidence, not all.
  • Call attention to physiological symptoms of guilt.
  • Sympathize: anyone might have done the same.
  • Suggest a more morally acceptable reason; minimize the moral seriousness.
  • Sympathize by condemning the victim, accomplice, or others who share blame.
  • Show understanding in urging truth; suggest the accuser may be exaggerating, or exaggerate the offense.
  • Place the subject at the scene; seek an admission of lying about an incidental detail.
  • Appeal to pride by flattery or challenge to honor.
  • Point out the futility of resisting and of continuing criminal behavior.
  • Ask about a detail rather than seeking a general admission.
  • With co-offenders, “play one against the other” (Mutt and Jeff method).

When guilt is uncertain

  • Ask if he knows why he is questioned; have him relate what he knows.
  • Get detailed activities before, during, and after the event.
  • Ask about suspicious facts casually; imply the answers are already known.
  • Refer to non-existent incriminating evidence to see if he explains it away.
  • Ask if he ever thought of committing the offense.
  • Offer of restitution in theft suggests guilt.
  • Ask if willing to take a lie detector test: the innocent usually agree; the guilty refuse or find excuses.
  • “All right, I’ll tell you what you want but I didn’t do it” likely indicates guilt.

General suggestions

  • Interview the victim, accuser, or discoverer first.
  • Be patient; make no promises when asked “What will happen if I tell the truth?”
  • Be skeptical of “conscience-stricken” confessions.
  • For repeated denials, first question about an unrelated similar offense.
  • Question an intelligent but uneducated suspect at a simple, child-like level.
