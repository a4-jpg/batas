Fire behavior

Thermal balance, dangerous behaviors, stages

Thermal balance and imbalance

Thermal balance
The rising movement or pattern of fire; normal behavior when the pattern is undisturbed.
Thermal imbalance
Abnormal movement of fire due to interference of foreign matter; it often confuses the investigator about the point of origin.

Dangerous behavior of fire

ConditionMeaning
BackdraftSudden, violent burning of heated gases in a confined area, as an explosion, from improper ventilation. Opening a door or window of an unventilated room sucks in oxygen and causes a combustion explosion.
FlashoverSudden ignition of accumulated radical gases from incomplete combustion, started by a spark or flash when temperature reaches flash point. Creates a fireball that can travel a hundred yards in seconds.
BitebackFire resists extinguishment and becomes stronger and bigger.
Flash fireDust explosion, e.g., a dust-covered metal post hit by lightning: the dust burns at once with a bright flash, then an explosion.

Three stages of fire

StageKey points
Incipient (initial)Normal room temperature; base 400–800/1000°F; ceiling about 200°F; pyrolysis products mostly water vapor and CO₂, with small amounts of CO and sulfides.
Free burningAdequate oxygen; accelerated pyrolysis; convection currents (thermal columns); base 800–1000/1330°F, ceiling 1200–1600°F; flame “crawls” up walls leaving burnt patterns (fire fingerprints); flashover occurs.
SmolderingFlame ceases, dense smoke and heat fill the room; oxygen 13% or below; more carbon monoxide (ignition temperature about 1125°F); ceiling 1000–1300°F; superheated fuel under pressure with little oxygen. Adding oxygen causes backdraft.
Suppression: slow the burningControl: keep it from spreadingExtinguishment: put it fully out

Five stages of fire

  1. Ignition: the four elements of the fire tetrahedron come together.
  2. Growth: a fire plume forms and entrains air.
  3. Flashover: transition from growth to fully developed; all exposed combustible surfaces ignite. Not a specific event like ignition.
  4. Fully developed: all combustibles in the compartment are involved.
  5. Decay: fuel is consumed and heat release declines.

Causes of fire

Natural, accidental, intentional, undetermined

Classification based on cause

Natural/providentialAccidentalIntentional/incendiaryUndetermined
Spontaneous heating
Chemical reaction causing auto-ignition of organic materials as heat rises gradually in a confined space; sun rays focused through glass acting as a convex lens.
Lightning
Static electricity of great magnitude. Hot bolt: longer, only ignites combustibles. Cold bolt: shorter, can splinter property or blow a structure apart; tremendous amperage and very high temperature.

Accidental causes

TermMeaning
Short circuitAccidental connection between two points at different potentials in a low-resistance circuit.
ArcingSustained luminous discharge between separated electrodes; current crosses a gap between two conductors.
SparkingIncandescent particles when two different potentials touch; during short circuits or welding.
Induced currentLine surge: sudden current increase burning insulation, exploding the fuse box or burning appliances.
Overheating of appliancesRising amperage damages insulation; gradual or rapid, internal or external.

Purely accidental causes also include negligence and other human error.

Intentional (incendiary) and undetermined

IntentionalDeliberately set where the person knows the fire should not be set. Preparations or traces of accelerant, plants and trailers point to intentional cause.
Accelerant
Highly flammable chemicals that facilitate flame propagation.
Plant
Preparing or gathering combustibles needed to start a fire.
Trailer
Preparing flammable substances to spread the fire.
Undetermined
The proper class whenever the cause cannot be proven.

Firefighting operations

Procedures, extinguishment methods, PASS, BFP rules

Procedures

StepMeaning
1 Pre-fire planningDeveloping systematic courses of action to meet fire protection objectives; establishing the SOP.
2 Evaluation / size-upOn-the-spot mental evaluation by the officer-in-charge: learn the facts (5Ws–1H), understand fatal fire behavior, know own manpower and equipment, choose the course (entry or rescue).
3 EvacuationMoving people, livestock and property away from the burning area.
4 EntryAccessing the structure, possibly by force: for firefighters and equipment, rescue, and ventilation.
5 RescueExtricating people and livestock to a secure place.
6 Exposure“Cover exposure”: securing nearby buildings so fire does not extend.
7 ConfinementRestricting fire to its place of origin and preventing spread between sections.
8 VentilationDisplacing toxic gases and heated atmosphere with outside air. Vertical: top to bottom. Cross/horizontal: opening windows if gases have not reached higher levels. Mechanical: smoke ejector.
9 SalvageProtecting property from preventable damage: remove materials outside and cover with tarpaulins (waterproofed cotton canvas).
10 ExtinguishmentPutting out the main body of fire by the four methods.
11 OverhaulDetailed check to prevent re-flash; extinguish embers to prevent rekindling.
12 Fire scene investigationInquiry into origin and cause.

Four methods of extinguishment

Temperature reduction
Cool the fire, usually with water, until fuel gives off too little vapor to burn.
Fuel removal
Stop liquid flow, prevent flammable gas production, remove solid fuel, or let the fuel burn out.
Oxygen dilution
Reduce oxygen with inert gases or separate oxygen from the fuel.
Chemical inhibition
Dry chemical and halon interrupt flame production; effective only on burning gas and liquid fuels, which cannot burn in smoldering mode.

PASS and BFP rules

PullAimSqueezeSweep

Pull the pin (press the inspection band); aim at the base of the fire; squeeze the handle above the carrying handle (release to stop); sweep sideways at the base, then probe for smoldering hot spots and reflash.

BFP: the Chief, BFP investigates fires with property damage exceeding ₱60 million. The Regional Director for Fire Protection appoints uniformed personnel from Fire Officer I to Senior Fire Officer IV in the regional office under RA 9263.

Detection systems & ladders

Detectors, sprinklers, ground and aerial ladders

Fire detection

A detection system discovers fire in its earliest phase and activates an alarm.

DetectorHow it works
Ionization (smoke)A radioactive source makes the air inside conductive; visible or invisible combustion products interrupt the current and trigger the alarm.
Photoelectric (smoke)Projected light beam for large areas; reflected beam or spot type for small areas.
Rate-of-rise (heat)Reacts to a rapid temperature increase; allows a range, activates when the permitted maximum is surpassed.
Fixed-temperature (heat)Preset to activate at a given temperature; a calibration screw lets an installer reset it to avoid false alarms.
Sprinkler systemPipes carry water to ceiling nozzles; heat opens the nozzles directly above the fire.

Ladders

Two categories: ground ladders (10–55 ft) and aerial ladders. Uses: rescue, stretching a line into a fire building, and ventilation access.

TypeDetails
Straight/hookOne section, 12–16 ft (common 14 ft).
ExtensionBed plus one or more fly ladders; up to 35 ft. With a stay pole: tormentors. Over 35 ft: Bangor ladder.
AtticReaches hard-to-reach openings; folds or collapses.
WallFor rescue when a placed ladder falls short; climb one storey at a time.
AerialOn a turntable, extends to 100 ft; 3 or 4 metal fly sections raised by hydraulic cables.
Bed / fly
Bed: lowest section of an extension ladder. Fly: top section.
Butt / heel
Butt: bottom end. Heel: the part touching the ground.
Halyard / pawl (dog)
Halyard: rope or cable raising the fly. Pawl: locks the fly to the bed.
Rung / top / hooks
Rung: climbing cross member. Hooks: hold over roof peaks, sills or walls (roof ladders).
Stops / guides
Stops: blocks that keep the fly from extending too far. Guides: metal strips that guide the fly.

Arson investigation

Stages of arson, fire language, telltales, smoke and flame colors

Stages in the commission of arson

StageHandout example
AttemptedRags soaked in gasoline placed by a wooden wall; offender about to light a match is chased away. Overt acts began, but not all acts of execution were done.
FrustratedRags soaked in kerosene set alight near the partition of an inhabited house but extinguished before any part burned. The handout also cites tools or furniture not attached to the building burning (U.S. v. Valdez).
ConsummatedPart of the building burned before the fire was put out; setting fire to a building’s contents is consummated even if no part burned.

Fire language

Alligatoring
Large scales indicate rapid, intense heat.
Calcination
Changes in plaster or gypsum walls: water driven from gypsum, paper surface charred off.
Clean burn
Surface pattern where soot has burned away.
Crazing
Glass cracking into small irregular segments.
Charring
Decomposition by heat driving off gases and vapors; char shrinks, cracks and blisters.
Spalling
Loss of tensile strength of concrete or brick, usually with color change; may chip.
Depth of char
Distance from original wood surface to unburned wood; estimates how long it was exposed.
Fusion
Change in form of metal, glass or plastic from high heat; melting.

Telltale signs of arson

  • Burned building: a large fire in a modern concrete or semi-concrete building on arrival.
  • Separate fires: two or more in one building are certainly suspicious.
  • Color of smoke and smoke marks: observe at the start; volume and residue help.
  • Size of fire: compare with alarm type, time received and arrival of the first apparatus.
  • Odor: gasoline, alcohol, kerosene and other flammable liquids.
  • Condition of contents: valuables removed, or merchandise replaced with out-of-style articles.
