Personality Assessment Methods

Objective, Projective & Behavioral — Presented 2025-11-21

01

Objective Methods of Personality Assessment

Objective Methods

Contain short-answer items for which the assessee's task is to select one response from two or more provided options. Usually administered by paper-and-pencil or computer. Scoring follows set procedures involving little, if any, judgment on the part of the scorer.

Format

Item Types & Scoring Reference

Objective personality tests may include items in a multiple-choice, true-false, or matching format. Unlike ability tests, a response is scored with reference to either:

  • [1]The personality characteristic(s) being measured
  • [2]The validity of the respondent's pattern of responses

If a respondent endorses items indicative of both the presence and absence of a trait — or items rarely endorsed by test-takers — the validity of the protocol is called into question and scrutiny may reveal an irregularity.

Advantages

Why Objective Tests Are Useful

Speed

Items can be answered quickly, allowing many items covering varied trait aspects.

Clear Instructions

Well-written items require little explanation — ideal for group and computerized administration.

Reliable Scoring

Items can be scored quickly by hand (template) or by computer with high consistency.

Fast Interpretation

Analysis may be nearly as fast as scoring, especially with custom software.

Limitation

How "Objective" Are They, Really?

The term objective originally referred to the fact that items had only one correct answer. However, personality tests depend on self-report, which introduces a distinct lack of objectivity:

  • Self-reports of likes/dislikes, agreements, behaviors can be subjective and biased.
  • Test-takers may fake good (present themselves favorably) or fake bad (exaggerate problems).
  • The term objective is best understood as a shorthand for a test format — short-answer with little room for scorer discretion — rather than a claim about the content's objectivity.
02

Projective Method

Projective Hypothesis

Holds that an individual supplies structure to unstructured stimuli in a manner consistent with the individual's own unique pattern of conscious and unconscious needs, fears, desires, impulses, conflicts, and ways of perceiving and responding.

Projective Method

A technique of personality assessment in which judgment of the assessee's personality is made on the basis of performance on a task that involves supplying some sort of structure to unstructured or incomplete stimuli.

Key Characteristics

What Makes Projective Tests Different

Indirect

Assessees are not directly asked to disclose information about themselves — they talk about "something else" (inkblots, pictures).

Faking Minimized

The ability — and inclination — to fake responses is greatly reduced on projective tasks.

Language-Flexible

Some tasks (e.g., drawings) require minimal language proficiency, suggesting cross-cultural potential.

Clinical Focus

Born from rejection of normative statistics; focused on the unique way an individual perceives and responds to ambiguous stimuli.

Inkblots as Projective Stimuli

The Rorschach Inkblot Test

Developed by Hermann Rorschach, who called it a "form interpretation test." Published in his 1921 monograph Psychodiagnostics. Consists of 10 bilaterally symmetrical inkblots on separate cards. Rorschach died at age 38, a year after publication.

Composition

The 10 Rorschach Cards

5 CARDS
Achromatic

Black-and-white only; no color.

2 CARDS
Black, White & Red

Include red inkblot areas in addition to black and white.

3 CARDS
Multicolored

Full color inkblots with varied hues.

There is no test manual included with the cards; no rationale for the color choices. Manuals were later developed, with Exner's Comprehensive System being the most widely used.

Administration

Three Phases of Rorschach Administration

  • [1]
    Initial Administration — Cards are presented one at a time (1–10). The test-taker answers "What might this be?" The examiner records verbatim responses, nonverbal gestures, response time, card position. No discussion during this phase.
  • [2]
    Inquiry — After all cards, the examiner asks questions to clarify what was seen and which inkblot features influenced the percept (perception). Provides data useful for scoring and interpretation. Also reveals whether original percepts are still seen.
  • [3]
    Testing the Limits — Optional third phase. Examiner restructures the situation with specific questions to gather additional personality data (e.g., can the person elaborate on details? respond with a new frame of reference? perform better with added structure?).
Scoring

Rorschach Scoring Categories

[1] LOCATION
Where on the inkblot?

Entire inkblot, large section, small section, minute detail, or white spaces.

[2] DETERMINANTS
What qualities drove it?

Form, color, shading, or movement the individual attributes to the inkblot.

[3] CONTENT
What was perceived?

Human figures, animal figures, anatomy, blood, clouds, X-rays, sexual responses, etc.

[4] POPULARITY
Common or rare?

Popular = frequently seen by the general population. Rare = infrequent responses.

[5] FORM LEVEL
How accurate?

How well the perceived image matches the actual inkblot — rated adequate/inadequate or good/poor.

Reliability & Validity

Psychometric Concerns & Developments

  • Split-half reliability is inappropriate because each card has a unique stimulus quality.
  • Test-retest reliability is problematic due to familiarity effects and because responses may reflect transient states, not enduring traits.
  • Exner's Comprehensive System improved inter-scorer reliability — McDowell & Acklin (1996) reported an overall mean agreement of 87%.
  • In 2011, the Rorschach Performance Assessment System (R-PAS) was published to leverage the Rorschach's strengths as a portable, complex behavioral task with standardized online scoring.
  • The Rorschach remains one of the most frequently used and taught psychological tests; widely used in forensic work and generally accepted by the courts.

Pictures as Projective Stimuli

Thematic Apperception Test (TAT)

Developed by Christiana D. Morgan & Henry A. Murray (1935) at the Harvard Psychological Clinic. The most widely used picture storytelling projective test. Originally designed to elicit fantasy material from patients in psychoanalysis. Consists of 31 cards (30 black-and-white picture cards + 1 blank card).

Administration

How the TAT Is Given

Test-takers are told it is a test of imagination. For each ambiguous picture they must create a story covering:

  • BEFOREWhat events led up to the scene in the picture?
  • NOWWhat is happening at that moment?
  • AFTERWhat will the outcome be?
  • INNERWhat are the thoughts and emotions of the people depicted?

For the blank card, the test-taker imagines a picture on it and creates a story. Card selection (typically 20) is tailored by age/gender. Apperception = to perceive in terms of past perceptions.

Interpretation

Murray's Key Interpretive Concepts

Need

Determinants of behavior arising from within the individual.

Press

Determinants of behavior arising from within the environment.

Thema

A unit of interaction between needs and press — the narrative unit of analysis.

The general principle is that the test-taker identifies with the protagonist and the protagonist's needs, conflicts, and environment reflect the test-taker's own concerns, hopes, fears, or desires.

Raw material for interpretation includes: (1) the stories as told, (2) notes on the manner of responding, and (3) notes on extra-test behavior and verbalizations.

Limitations

Psychometric Concerns with the TAT

  • Different cards have different stimulus "pulls" (latent stimulus demands), making inter-card reliability difficult to determine.
  • Clinicians vary significantly in administration, scoring, and interpretation — limiting standardization.
  • Little direct evidence linking TAT stories to real-life behavior.
  • Results can be manipulated; easy to fake.
  • McClelland et al. (1989): self-report measures tap self-attributed motives; TAT taps implicit motives — non-conscious behavioral influences acquired from experience.
  • Despite limitations, the TAT remains commonly used because the underlying idea is intuitive.
Other Picture Tests

TAT-Like Instruments

TestDescription
Social Situation Picture Test (SSPT) Developed 1932. Projective instrument designed for use with juvenile delinquents.
Senior Apperception Technique (SAT) Features pictures of seniors; assumes elderly examinees will identify more strongly with age-matched images.
Hand Test 9 cards with pictures of hands + 1 blank card. Test-taker describes what the hands might be doing. Scored across 24 categories (affection, aggression, dependence, etc.).
Apperceptive Personality Test (APT) 8 cards with recognizable people in everyday settings. After a story, test-takers answer multiple-choice questions — introduces objectivity into scoring.
Hand Test Scoring

Aggression Direction & Reaction Categories

Intropunitive

Aggression turned inward; self-directed.

Extrapunitive

Aggression directed outward; expressed toward others.

Impunitive

Aggression evaded; glossed over to avoid the situation.

Reactions also grouped into: Obstacle Dominance (focus on frustrating barrier), Ego Defense (protect the frustrated person), and Need Persistence (focus on solving the problem). A Group Conformity Rating (GCR) shows how typical responses are relative to the standardization group.

Words as Projective Stimuli

Overview

Semi-Structured Verbal Techniques

Projective techniques that use words or open-ended phrases are called semi-structured — they allow varied responses but provide a framework. The two best-known types are Word Association Tests and Sentence Completion Tests.

Word Association

A task in which an assessee verbalizes the first word that comes to mind in response to a stimulus word. Responses are analyzed based on content, reaction time, popularity, and test-retest consistency.

Historical Development

Word Association Tests — Key Figures

Figure / YearContribution
Galton (1879) First to investigate word association. Presented unrelated words; recorded responses and response time.
Cattell & Bryant (1889) First to use printed stimulus word cards.
Jung (1910) Proposed that selecting key "conflict words" allows word association to be used for psychodiagnostic purposes. Inspired Rapaport et al.
Rapaport et al. (1945–46) Developed a 60-word test at the Menninger Clinic with 3 phases: (1) initial response + timing, (2) replication + timing, (3) inquiry into stimulus-response relationships.
Kent-Rosanoff Test 100 neutral stimulus words; frequency tables from 1,000 normal adults. Psychiatric patients showed lower frequency of popular responses than the normal standardization group.
Rapaport Word Categories

60-Word Test — Neutral vs. Traumatic Words

Neutral Words

e.g., chair, book, water, dance, taxi — emotionally benign.

Traumatic Words

e.g., love, mother, father, suicide, fire, breast — words likely to touch sensitive personal material or attract associative disturbances.

Sentence Completion

A task in which the assessee is asked to finish an incomplete sentence or phrase. Responses are analyzed for themes, attitudes, beliefs, and personality traits.

Sentence Completion Tests

Major Instruments

TestKey Features
Washington University Sentence Completion Test Based on Loevinger's theory of self-concept development (theoretically linked).
Rotter Incomplete Sentences Blank Most popular among clinicians. Grade 9 through adulthood. Three levels: High School, College, Adult. 40 items scored on a 7-point scale from need for therapy to extremely good adjustment. Interpreted across: family attitudes, social/sexual attitudes, general attitudes, and character traits.
Sacks Sentence Completion Test (SSCT) Multiple sets of sentence stems with different themes. Taps deeper levels of the psyche and unconscious. Reveals personality traits, coping mechanisms, and emotional states.

Sounds as Projective Stimuli

Auditory Projective Tests

Major Instruments & Figures

Test / NameDescription
Verbal Summator / Tautophone B.F. Skinner developed recorded muffled vowel-like sounds; believed it could "snare complexes." Rosenzweig & Shakow renamed it the tautophone (from Greek: "repeating the same") and did research with it.
Auditory Apperception Test (AAT) Test-taker creates a story based on three sounds. Examiner analyzes content and manner of response to identify unconscious themes.
Azzageddi Test Created by Henry Murray; named after a Herman Melville character. Presents spoken paragraphs (not single words) — responses analyzed for unconscious motivations, attitudes, and personality traits.

Overall, auditory projective responses lacked the complexity and richness of inkblot, picture, or drawing responses, and no available scoring system proved very satisfactory.

The Production of Figure Drawings

Figure Drawing Test

A projective technique whereby the assessee produces a drawing that is analyzed on the basis of its content and related variables. Quick to administer (individually or in groups by non-clinicians); requires only pencil and paper. Classic reference: Personality Projection in the Drawing of the Human Figure by Karen Machover (1949).

Three Main Drawing Tests

DAP, HTP, and KFD

DAP
Draw A Person Test

Draw a person (8½×11 paper, pencil). Then draw a person of the opposite sex. Clinician follows up with questions: "Tell me a story about this figure," "What is the person doing?", "How is the person feeling?" Hypotheses formed from placement, size, pencil pressure, symmetry, shading, erasures, facial expression, posture, clothing.

HTP
House-Tree-Person Test

Buck (1948). Draw a house, a tree, and a person. How house and tree are represented is considered symbolically significant — analogous to how human figure features reflect psychological functioning.

KFD
Kinetic Family Drawing

"Draw a picture of everyone in your family, including you, DOING something. Try to draw whole people, not cartoons or stick people." Detailed inquiry follows: identify figures, describe relationships, what they're doing and why. Especially valuable for understanding the examinee in relation to their family.

Interpretation Focus

What Figure Drawings Reveal (Per Emanuel Hammer)

People project their self-image or self-concept in figure drawings (also in dreams and paintings). Hammer identified commonalities in drawings by persons from certain diagnostic groups.

  • Placement on paper = how the individual functions within the environment.
  • Characteristics of the drawn figure = reflect self-concept and psychological traits.
  • Drawings are both a reflection of and a window into personality.
Related Instruments

Kinetic Drawing Variations

Kinetic School Drawing (KSD)

School adaptation of the KFD; examines the child's relationship to the school environment.

Kinetic Drawing System (KDS)

Combines aspects of the KFD and KSD into a unified assessment tool.

Behavioral Assessment Methods

Behavioral Assessment

An approach to personality assessment focused on direct observation and measurement of overt behavior in specific contexts. Rather than inferring underlying traits, it identifies specific behaviors, their antecedents, and their consequences.

Core Features

What Makes Behavioral Assessment Distinct

Direct Observation

Behavior is observed and recorded as it occurs in natural or analogue settings.

Context-Specific

Focuses on how behavior varies across different situations and environments.

Overt Focus

Emphasizes observable, measurable behaviors rather than inferred internal states.

Antecedent-Behavior-Consequence

Examines what triggers the behavior and what follows it (ABC model).

The Dichotomy & A Perspective

Objective vs. Projective Dichotomy

Comparing the Two Major Approaches

DimensionObjective MethodsProjective Methods
Stimuli Structured; defined items with set response options Unstructured or ambiguous; open-ended
Scoring Standardized; little scorer judgment needed Requires clinical expertise; higher subjectivity
Faking More vulnerable to faking good/bad Faking is greatly minimized
Reliability Generally high (standardized procedures) Variable; inter-scorer reliability challenges
Cultural Use May be culturally biased in content Some promise of cross-cultural utility (esp. non-verbal)
Speed Fast to administer and score More time-intensive; requires clinical training
A Perspective

Academics vs. Practitioners

An ongoing debate exists between academic researchers (who emphasize psychometric rigor — reliability, validity, standardization) and clinicians (who value the rich, in-depth, individualized information projective tests provide).

  • Researchers tend to favor objective methods for their measurable psychometric properties.
  • Clinicians prize projective methods for the unique window they provide into unconscious processes and personality dynamics.
  • Both approaches are complementary — a comprehensive assessment often uses multiple methods to form a fuller picture of the individual.

Key Takeaways — Lesson 11

  • Objective methods use structured short-answer formats (true-false, multiple-choice); scored with minimal judgment; vulnerable to self-report biases like faking good/bad.
  • The term "objective" describes the test format, not the objectivity of the content — self-report is inherently subjective.
  • Projective methods rest on the Projective Hypothesis: individuals impose their own psychological patterns onto ambiguous stimuli.
  • Projective tests are indirect — assessees respond to inkblots/pictures, not direct questions about themselves — minimizing faking.
  • The Rorschach consists of 10 bilateral inkblots; administered in 3 phases: Initial Administration → Inquiry → Testing the Limits.
  • Rorschach scoring categories: Location, Determinants, Content, Popularity, Form Level.
  • Exner's Comprehensive System is the most widely used Rorschach scoring method; R-PAS (2011) is the newest standardized system.
  • TAT (Morgan & Murray, 1935): 31 cards; test-takers construct stories covering before, now, after, and inner states. Interpreted via Need, Press, and Thema.
  • Other picture tests: SSPT (juvenile delinquents), SAT (seniors), Hand Test (24 scoring categories), APT (adds multiple-choice for objectivity).
  • Word Association: Galton (1879) started it; Jung (1910) proposed psychodiagnostic use; Rapaport et al. created a 60-word neutral/traumatic test.
  • Sentence Completion: semi-structured; Rotter ISB (most popular, 7-point scale) and SSCT (multiple theme sets) are key instruments.
  • Auditory projective tests (Tautophone, AAT, Azzageddi) largely failed — responses lacked richness and scoring was unsatisfactory.
  • Figure Drawing Tests (DAP, HTP, KFD) project self-concept; analyzed via placement, size, pressure, shading, erasures, and figure characteristics.
  • Behavioral assessment focuses on direct observation of overt behavior, its antecedents, and its consequences — context-specific and empirical.
  • No single method is superior; a comprehensive personality assessment typically combines objective, projective, and behavioral approaches.
