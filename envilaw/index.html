The environment

Definition, three categories, four spheres

What is environment?

DefinitionThe surrounding conditions that affect the lifestyle of living things. It includes natural and manmade things and covers both abiotic (physical, non-living) and biotic (living) parts.
  • The slides trace the word to the French “Environia”, meaning to surround.
  • Environment and organisms are two dynamic, complex components of nature; humans interact with it more vigorously than other beings.
  • Human life is affected by environment, so human life differs between regions.

Three categories of environment

Natural
Everything that exists naturally: landforms, climate, soils, minerals, sunlight.
Built
Human-made surroundings for human activity: buildings, parks, neighborhoods, cities, plus infrastructure such as water supply and energy networks.
Social
Social networks and relationships between individuals in a context (neighborhood, school) that may influence behavior (McNeill et al., 2006).

Four components (spheres)

SphereRepresentsKey facts
LithosphereRocksOutermost layer (crust), made of minerals, up to 100 km deep; terrestrial and oceanic crust; main component is the tectonic plates.
HydrosphereWaterAll water bodies; covers 70% of Earth’s surface. 97.5% is ocean salt water, 2.5% freshwater: 30.8% groundwater, 68.9% frozen (glaciers), 0.3% in rivers, reservoirs and lakes (easily accessible).
AtmosphereAir78.08% nitrogen, 20.95% oxygen, 0.93% argon, 0.038% CO₂, traces of hydrogen, helium, noble gases; water vapour varies.
BiosphereLifeAll regions where life exists, the sum of living matter (biomass/biota). Term coined by geologist Edward Suess.

Adaptation & interaction

How organisms and human societies adjust to environment

Three types of adaptation

Heraclitus: “the only constant is change.” Organisms that cannot adapt die. Genetic change is the basis of all adaptations; types are classified by how the change is expressed.

TypeMeaningExamples
StructuralAppearance shaped by environmentDesert fox large ears (shed heat), Arctic fox small ears (retain heat); seal flippers; raccoon flexible digits; polar bear white, jaguar spotted; corky bark vs. wildfire.
PhysiologicalBody chemistry and metabolism; usually invisible; found by lab testsKangaroo rat efficient kidneys; mosquito saliva anticoagulant; toxins in plant leaves.
BehavioralHow an organism actsHibernation, migration, night activity in deserts, killdeer feigning injury, bowerbird mating. Humans: cultural adaptation, a subset of behavioral.

Human–environment interactions

DefinitionInteractions between the human social system and the rest of the ecosystem. Both are complex adaptive systems (Marten, 2001): complex (many parts and connections), adaptive (feedback structures that promote survival).
Ecosystem
Area where plants, animals, other organisms, weather and landscape work together as a “bubble of life”.
Human social system
Shaped by population size, social organization, values, technology, wealth, education, knowledge; society type affects attitudes toward nature.
Ecosystem services
Benefits people obtain: water, timber, food, energy, information, farmland. Essential to well-being.
Millennium Ecosystem Assessment
UN-supported programme launched 2001 on links between ecosystem services and human well-being.
MEA ecosystem servicesExamples
ProvisioningFood, fresh water, wood and fiber, fuel
RegulatingClimate, flood and disease regulation; water purification
CulturalAesthetic, spiritual, educational, recreational
SupportingNutrient cycling, soil formation, primary production

Constituents of well-being: security; basic material for a good life; health; good social relations; freedom of choice and action.

Coevolution and coadaptation

Coevolution
Two or more species exert selective pressure on each other and evolve in response, so the selective environment keeps changing.
Coadaptation
Reciprocal adaptation of two species (also individuals, genotypes).
For humans
Never-ending mutual adjustment: people change the environment and storms, earthquakes force people to react.
Biotic–abiotic link
All biotic components depend on each other; plants make food from solar energy (abiotic + biotic interaction).

Issues & solutions

Population, acid rain, disasters, global warming

Impacts of population growth

Industrialization, burning fossil fuels, use of non-renewable resources, pollution, increased CO₂.

Acid rainBurning fossil fuels releases sulphates and nitrates that mix with water vapour to form sulphuric and nitric acid. Lakes and ponds can become so acidified that no life survives.

Disasters: more than 1.4 million died in natural disasters over 50 years (earthquakes the biggest killers); 1998 losses exceeded US$93 billion.

Six changes in the man–environment relationship

ChallengePoint
1 Population explosionPressure on resources; growth leads to poverty and lower environmental standards.
2 WaterRestore water quality and use non-polluted water rationally.
3 AgricultureGrow without damage; over-cultivation causes nutrient deficiency, lost organic matter, salinity, damaged soil structure.
4 Soil erosionRestore land; manage land, water and forest together.
5 ForestsDepleting fast for timber and farmland; restore via social forestry and afforestation.
6 PollutionOverexploitation, disturbed biogeochemical cycles, GHG emissions, hazardous chemicals, nuclear arsenals, radioactive waste, biotech misuse.

Ways of tackling the problems

  • Population control; optimum use of natural resources; conserve biodiversity.
  • Public awareness; top priority for environmental protection; eco-friendly technology.
  • Sustainable agriculture, bio-fertilisers, minimal pesticides and insecticides.
  • Afforestation of wasteland; biotechnology to clean up hazardous wastes.

Global warming and environmental issues

Global warming is the rise in average near-surface air temperature over the past one to two centuries. Slide issue lists include:

Overpopulation
Biocapacity, carrying capacity, overshoot, urbanization, waste. I = P × A × T: impact = population × affluence (per capita) × technology (resource-depleting, polluting).
Hydrology
Science of occurrence, distribution, movement and properties of Earth’s waters and their relation to environment; flooding, landslide.
Intensive farming
Monoculture, overgrazing, slash and burn, irrigation, nutrient pollution, pesticide drift.
Land use
Desertification, habitat destruction, urban sprawl, urban heat island.
Natural disasters
Floods, hurricanes, tornadoes, eruptions, earthquakes, tsunamis.
Climate change effects
Global warming and dimming, sea level rise, greenhouse gas, ocean acidification, thermohaline shutdown.
Areas of concern (slide): air and water quality; global climate change; agriculture; biodiversity and species protection; pesticides and hazardous chemicals; waste management and remediation of contaminated land; management of public lands and natural resources.

International environmental law

Chapter 7: principles, treaties, conventions

Environmental law and its principles

DefinitionA network of treaties, statutes, regulations, common and customary laws addressing the effects of human activity on the natural environment, to reduce impacts on nature itself and on humanity.
EnvironmentalismEcologyConservationStewardshipResponsibility & sustainability

Treaties, conventions, protocols

InstrumentKey points
Basel Convention (1992)Control of Transboundary Movements of Hazardous Wastes and their Disposal. Most comprehensive global agreement on hazardous wastes: generation and management, transboundary movements, disposal.
Convention on Biological DiversityBiodiversity damage is handled comprehensively, not species by species. Objectives: (1) conservation, (2) sustainable use of components, (3) fair and equitable sharing of benefits from genetic resources.
CITESEnsures international trade in wild animals and plants does not threaten their survival (e.g., tigers, elephants).
Bonn Convention (Migratory Species)Conserves terrestrial, marine and avian migratory species throughout their range: bats, cetaceans, seals, waterbirds, albatrosses and petrels, gorillas.
Kyoto ProtocolDefined three innovative “flexibility” mechanisms letting Parties cut emissions or remove carbon cost-effectively in other countries.
Outside the slides: Basel was adopted in 1989 and took effect in 1992; Kyoto’s three mechanisms are emissions trading, the Clean Development Mechanism and Joint Implementation.

Philippine environmental laws

Chapter 8: statutes, decrees and protection guidelines

Ecology and the Three Rs

“Economy” and “ecology” both come from the Greek oikos, “home”. Ecology studies interactions between organisms and their environment.

Reduce›Reuse›Recycle

The waste hierarchy is the order of priority for cutting waste. Reduce: less produced and consumed means less to handle. Reuse: repurpose (e.g., buildings made from shipping containers). Recycle: turn waste back into raw material; needs a facility to process it.

Laws at a glance

LawWhat it does
RA 9003Ecological Solid Waste Management Act of 2000. Segregation and collection at the barangay level for biodegradable, compostable and reusable wastes.
RA 9275Philippine Clean Water Act of 2004; comprehensive water quality management.
RA 8749Philippine Clean Air Act of 1999. Right to a balanced, healthful ecology; LGUs hold primary responsibility; cleanup is area-based; polluters must pay; environment is everyone’s concern.
RA 6969Toxic Substances and Hazardous and Nuclear Wastes Control Act; regulates chemicals; bars entry, even in transit, of hazardous and nuclear wastes.
RA 8550Fisheries Code; protection, conservation and management of fisheries; allowable fishing methods in coastal waters.
RA 9729Climate Change Act of 2009; mainstreams climate change in policy; creates the Climate Change Commission.
PD 984Pollution Control Law (revises RA 3931); policy to prevent, abate and control water, air and land pollution.
PD 1181Vehicular pollution; maximum emissions of air pollutants from vehicles; supplements PD 984.
PD 600 (amended by PD 979)Prohibits discharge of oil, noxious liquids and other harmful substances into inland and territorial waters.
PD 825Penalty for improper disposal of garbage and other uncleanliness.
PD 953Tree planting and penalties for unauthorized cutting or damage of trees. Plant: (1) riverbank landowners, ≥5 m from the bank; (2) subdivision owners, open spaces and along roads; (3) holders of government forest or grazing permits with a river or creek, ≥20 m from each bank.
PD 1219Coral Resources Development and Conservation Decree.

Environmental protection

Slides define it as action to remedy or prevent damage to surroundings or natural resources, reduce risk, or use resources more efficiently (energy saving, renewables); as protection from physical, chemical, biological and other adverse effects of plans, programs and economic activity; and describe land unsuitable for buildings (flood plain, erosion, steep slopes, organic soils, high water table, natural heritage features).

  1. Sustainable development through protection.
  2. Effective resource use, energy saving, fewer exhaust emissions.
  3. Clean production, pollution prevention, law compliance.
  4. Raise awareness through training.
  5. Promote harmony through information training.
  6. Seek perfection through continued improvement.

Prevention & treatment of pollutants

Wastewater, exhaust gases, solid waste

Treatment of wastewater

DefinitionAlso called sewage treatment: removing impurities from wastewater before it reaches aquifers or bodies of water. Clean vs. polluted depends on the impurities and the intended use.
StepInfographic summary
1Sewage pumped to the lifting station, chemically treated, sent for separation.
2Separation removes solids larger than 13 mm; sent to a refuse facility and buried.
3Primary settling: heavier material sinks and is scraped away; fermented 30 days, used as fertiliser.
4Effluent to bio-reactors for 9 hours; bacteria break down harmful matter.
5Secondary clarifiers; bacteria keep cleaning to a drinkable level.
6Chlorine treatment, then over a weir into the water supply.

Plant diagrams also show a bar screen, equalization tank, primary and secondary settling, aeration tank, chlorine contact tank, and sludge drying beds.

Treatment of exhaust gases and solid waste

Exhaust gases
The captive power plant uses a state-of-the-art CFB (circulating fluidized-bed) boiler and adds limestone during combustion; desulphurization rate over 85–87%.
Solid waste
Comprehensive utilization: burning in the captive boiler, drying fume, and supplying cement manufacturers as raw material.
