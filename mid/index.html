Counseling foundations & ethics

Definitions, models, the counselor as a person, values, and ethical practice.

Part A · The field

Counseling vs. psychotherapy

Counseling

  • “Counselor” means advisor: two people working together to solve a problem.
  • Relatively brief treatment focused mainly on behavior.
  • Targets a particular symptom or problematic situation; offers suggestions and advice.
  • Short-term solution for present issues; action and behavior focused; secondary process; offers guidance and support.

Psychotherapy

  • Generally longer-term treatment.
  • Gains insight into chronic physical and emotional problems.
  • Focuses on thought processes and how past events cause problems in the present.
  • Long-term solution for recurring problems; feeling and experience focused; primary process; talk therapy and other strategies.

Types under psychotherapy include cognitive behavioral therapy, dialectical behavior therapy, and psychoanalytic psychotherapy.

Part B · The counselor as a person

The counselor as therapist

Counseling is an intimate, person-to-person learning process. The counselor's authenticity is central to client growth.

Authenticity matters

  • Hiding behind roles or relying only on techniques limits effectiveness.
  • Genuineness and appropriate self-disclosure foster honesty and trust.

The therapist as a person

  • The therapist's personality and character strongly influence outcomes.
  • Clients value the counselor's person more than specific techniques.

Therapeutic relationship

  • Alliance, empathy, and client agency drive change.
  • Techniques help only when they support the relationship.

Research evidence

  • Wampold (2001): relationship factors outweigh techniques.
  • Norcross & Lambert (2019): balance methods with strong relationships.
  • Norcross & Cooper (2021): respect the client's culture, values, and preferences.

Personal characteristics of an effective counselor

Twelve traits from Corey. Click a card to see it clearly; they are grouped as they appear in the slides.

1. Open to change
Willingness and courage to leave the security of the known if not satisfied with how they are.
2. Life-oriented choices
Aware of early decisions about self, others, and the world; not victims of them; willing to revise; committed to living fully.
3. Authentic, sincere, honest
Do not hide behind rigid roles or facades; personal and professional lives are congruent.
4. Sense of humor
Keep life in perspective; can laugh at their own foibles and contradictions.
5. Admit mistakes
Do not dismiss errors lightly, yet do not dwell on misery.
6. Live in the present
Not riveted to the past or fixated on the future; present with others in the “now.”
7. Appreciate culture
Aware of how their own culture affects them; respect diversity of values, class, race, orientation, gender.
8. Sincere interest in others
Based on respect, care, trust, and real valuing of others.
9. Good interpersonal skills
Enter others' worlds without getting lost; collaborate toward consensual goals.
10. Involved in and derive meaning from work
Accept rewards of their work but are not slaves to it.
11. Passionate
Courage to pursue dreams and passions; radiate energy.
12. Healthy boundaries
Fully present for clients but do not carry clients' problems into leisure hours; know how to say no.

Care for the counselor

  • Counselors benefit from experiencing therapy as clients (Norcross, 2005; Orlinsky et al., 2005); most mental health professionals undergo personal therapy, often several times.
  • Benefits: self-awareness, self-acceptance, self-care, symptom reduction, personal growth.

How personal therapy helps professionally

  • Training model: observe and learn from experienced therapists.
  • Skill enhancement: improves interpersonal and therapeutic skills.
  • Stress management: copes with clinical demands.
  • Centrality of warmth, empathy, relationships; insight into being a client; patience; handling transference and countertransference.

Personal blocks

  • Counselors face their own struggles (anger, depression, intimacy, death).
  • Need not be conflict-free, but must be aware of issues and their effect on work.
  • Counselors can only take clients as far as they themselves have gone.
ResearchGraduate clinicians with personal therapy show greater confidence and better agreement with clients on goals and tasks (Gold & Hilsenroth, 2009). Periodic personal therapy is the ideal self-care strategy (Wise & Barnett, 2016); it prevents arrogance and supports humility.
Part C · Values & ethics

The role of values in counseling

Values shape personal and professional actions; they affect assessments, goals, interventions, topics, evaluation, and interpretation of client issues.

Counselor's role

  • Total objectivity is impossible, but avoid imposing values.
  • Create a safe, accepting space for clients to explore their own values.
  • Help clients clarify goals, make informed choices, take responsibility.
  • Respect clients' right to different values, especially across cultures.

Bracketing

  • Managing personal values so they do not interfere in counseling.
  • Set aside your beliefs to work with diverse clients (Kocet & Herlihy, 2014).
  • Honor client worldviews to avoid further discrimination.

Value imposition

  • Counselor tries to define the client's values, beliefs, or behaviors.
  • Can be direct or indirect, intentional or unintentional.
  • ACA (2014) Standard A.4.b: do not impose personal values; respect diversity.

Ethical practice

  • Seek training to prevent imposing values; explore your values through personal therapy or self-reflection.
  • Value conflict: seek supervision or consultation.
  • Referral is ethical only for lack of competence or skills, not for value disagreement.
Client-centered focusClients are vulnerable; judgment or rejection causes harm. Counseling is about the client's values and needs, not the counselor's. Keep professional and personal life separate.

Mandatory, aspirational, and positive ethics

Mandatory ethics
The minimum level of professional practice.
Aspirational ethics
Doing what is in the best interests of clients.
Positive ethics
Practitioners who want to do their best for clients rather than merely meet minimum ethical and legal standards to stay out of trouble (Knapp & VandeCreek, 2012).

Clients come first

Counselors must be aware of personal needs, unfinished business, potential personal problems, and countertransference.

Key question“Whose needs are being met — my client's or my own?”

How to make ethical decisions

Corey et al. (2024), eight steps:

  1. Identify the problem or dilemma. Gather information; decide if it is ethical, legal, professional, clinical, or moral.
  2. Identify the potential issues. Evaluate rights, responsibilities, and welfare of everyone involved.
  3. Look at the relevant ethics codes. Check whether your values are consistent with or in conflict with them.
  4. Consider applicable laws and regulations and their bearing on the dilemma.
  5. Seek consultation from more than one source and document the suggestions in the client's record.
  6. Brainstorm possible courses of action. Include the client; document the discussion.
  7. Enumerate the consequences of each decision for the client.
  8. Decide, implement, follow up. Evaluate outcomes and document reasons and evaluation measures.

Informed consent

An ethical and legal requirement, regardless of theoretical framework; the foundation of the therapeutic alliance. It ensures clients are informed, can make autonomous decisions, and become active partners.

Key elements

  • Goals of counseling
  • Responsibilities of counselor and client
  • Confidentiality limits and exceptions
  • Legal and ethical parameters
  • Counselor's qualifications and background
  • Fees and services
  • Expected length of therapy
  • Benefits, risks, supervision and consultation

Process and format

  • Ongoing: begins at the first session and continues throughout.
  • Balance too much vs. too little information.
  • Clients must know confidentiality limits before disclosures (e.g., minors, sensitive topics).
  • Written, oral, or both; oral consent must be documented.
  • Written info protects both parties and allows review.
  • Technology: email, texts, and home or work communication carry risks; limit tech to scheduling and basic info.

Confidentiality and its exceptions

Exceptions (Remley & Herlihy, 2020):

  • Client under age 16 is the victim of incest, rape, child abuse, or another crime.
  • The therapist determines the client needs hospitalization.
  • Information is made an issue in a court action.
  • Clients request release of their records to themselves or a third party.

Multiple relationships

Dual or multiple relationships (sexual or nonsexual) occur when counselors assume two or more roles with a client, simultaneously or sequentially. ACA (2014) uses the term nonprofessional relationships.

Why they are problematic

  • Pervasive; difficult to recognize; unavoidable at times
  • Potentially harmful, but not always
  • Can be beneficial
  • Subject of conflicting expert advice

Minimizing harm

  • Set healthy boundaries early; informed consent throughout.
  • Involve clients in discussion and decisions; document.
  • Consult colleagues to keep objectivity.
  • Work under supervision when risk is high; document it.
  • Self-monitor: whose needs are being met?
Boundary crossingA departure from commonly accepted practice that could potentially benefit the client. Example: attending a client's wedding.

How to be an ethical counselor

  • Knowing the code is essential but does not make decisions for you; applying it takes sensitivity and judgment.
  • Counselors take responsibility for acting in the clients' best interests.
  • Ethical questions evolve; reexamine them throughout your career.
  • Training, supervision, and peer consultation help with dilemmas.
  • Ethical decision-making is ongoing, reflective, and consultative. Becoming ethical is a lifelong journey, not a final destination.

Psychoanalytic psychotherapy

Freud's personality theory, defenses, development, ego psychology, and the analytic process.

Part A · Theory

View of human nature

  • Behavior is shaped by irrational forces, unconscious motives, and biological and instinctual drives.
  • Development follows psychosexual stages in the first six years.
  • Life instincts (Eros): survival, growth, development, creativity; all pleasurable acts that gain pleasure and avoid pain.
  • Death instincts: unconscious wishes to self-destruct or harm others.
  • Sexual and aggressive drives are the two central motivators.

Structure of personality

Id

  • Original system, present at birth; source of instincts and psychic energy.
  • Pleasure principle: immediate tension release.
  • Illogical, amoral, impulsive, unconscious; never matures: the “spoiled brat.”

Ego

  • Contact with external reality; the “executive.”
  • Mediates between id and environment.
  • Reality principle: realistic, logical, planful.
  • Distinguishes subjective reality (id) from objective reality.

Superego

  • “Judicial branch”; internalized parental and societal values.
  • Strives for perfection, not pleasure; inhibits id impulses.
  • Rewards: pride, self-love. Punishments: guilt, inferiority.

The unconscious

Stores experiences, memories, repressed material; contains needs and motives outside awareness; the root of most psychological functioning.

Clinical evidence

  • Dreams (symbolic of wishes and conflicts)
  • Slips of the tongue; forgetting
  • Posthypnotic suggestions
  • Free association material
  • Projective techniques
  • Psychotic symptoms with symbolic meaning

Role in therapy

  • Goal: make unconscious motives conscious, allowing personal choice.
  • Unconscious processes are the root of neurotic symptoms.
  • Cure: uncover meaning of symptoms; not just intellectual insight but working through resistance and transference.

Anxiety

Dread from repressed feelings, memories, desires or experiences; a tension that motivates action; arises from conflict among id, ego, and superego over psychic energy; functions as a warning signal of danger.

Reality anxiety
Fear of danger from the external world; proportional to the actual threat.
Neurotic anxiety
Fear that instincts will get out of control and lead to punishment; ego struggles to restrain the id.
Moral anxiety
Fear of one's own conscience; guilt for acting against one's moral code.

Ego-defense mechanisms

  • Purpose: cope with anxiety and protect the ego from being overwhelmed; normal and adaptive if used flexibly, not as an avoidance lifestyle.
  • They deny or distort reality and operate unconsciously.
  • Use varies with developmental level and degree of anxiety.
DefenseDefinitionUse / example
RepressionThreatening or painful thoughts and feelings are excluded from awareness.Most important process; basis of many other defenses and neuroses. Most painful events of the first five or six years are buried yet influence later behavior.
Denial“Closing one's eyes” to a threatening aspect of reality.Simplest defense; distorts what one thinks, feels, or perceives. Like repression but operates at preconscious and conscious levels.
Reaction formationActively expressing the opposite impulse of a threatening one.Conceal hate with a facade of love; be extremely nice while harboring negative reactions; mask cruelty with excessive kindness.
ProjectionAttributing to others one's own unacceptable desires and impulses.Self-deception: impulses are “possessed by those people out there, but not by me.”
DisplacementDirecting energy toward another object or person when the original one is inaccessible.Shift from a threatening object to a “safer target”: the meek man intimidated by his boss unloads hostility on his children.
RationalizationManufacturing “good” reasons to explain away a bruised ego.Softens disappointment: not getting a job, one decides they did not really want it anyway.
SublimationDiverting sexual or aggressive energy into other channels.Socially acceptable, even admirable, outlets such as athletics.
RegressionGoing back to an earlier phase of development with fewer demands.Frightened children weep, cling to the teacher, thumb-suck, hide.
IntrojectionTaking in and “swallowing” the values and standards of others.Positive: absorbing parental values. Negative: prisoners accepting values of the enemy through identification with the aggressor.
IdentificationIdentifying with successful causes, organizations, or people so you are perceived as worthwhile.Enhances self-worth; part of learning gender roles; defensive when one feels inferior.
CompensationMasking perceived weaknesses or developing positive traits to make up for limitations.“Don't see how I am inferior; see my accomplishments.”

Development of personality

The first six years are the foundation of later personality. Unmet needs at early stages lead to fixation and immature adult behavior.

OralDifficulty trusting self and others, fear of intimacy, low self-esteem
AnalProblems expressing anger, denial of personal power, lack of autonomy
PhallicInability to accept sexuality; problems with self-acceptance as male or female
Part B · Ego psychology & object relations

Anna Freud (1895–1982)

  • Wrote on normal and disturbed children; expanded psychoanalysis to maturation beyond sexual and aggressive drives.
  • Showed growth toward ego development: from egocentric focus to seeing peers as real people; from dependence to self-mastery.
  • Treated both ego and id. Book: The Ego and the Mechanisms of Defense (1936).
  • Described 10 known defenses and added two: identification with the aggressor and altruism (helping others to avoid feelings of helplessness).
  • Saw defenses as adaptive and maladaptive, and as defenses against external reality, not just internal drives.

Erik Erikson (1902–1994)

Student of Anna Freud; added psychosocial stages across the life span. Each stage is a crisis or developmental task; unresolved crises cause difficulty later. Unlike Freud's, the stages continue throughout life. Influenced by his work with children and adolescents and the identity crisis.

Object relations psychology

  • Focus: the child's relationships with significant others (love objects), especially the mother.
  • How the child internalizes these relationships, consciously or unconsciously; they shape adult personality and later interactions.
  • Concerned with intrapsychic processes, not only external behavior.
  • Key concept: individuation, the child's separation from the mother to become independent.
  • Differs from Freud's drives and stages by highlighting relationships.
Part C · The therapeutic process

Goals of psychoanalytic therapy

  • Help clients cope with and solve their own problems; reduce symptoms and resolve conflicts.
  • Make the unconscious conscious. Strengthen the ego so behavior is guided by reality, not instincts or guilt.
  • Outcome: modification of personality and character structure; self-understanding beyond symptom relief.
  • Reconstruct and analyze childhood experiences; achieve insight with emotional experience, not just intellectual understanding.

Therapist's function and role

Classical stance

  • Anonymous, neutral (blank screen).
  • Avoids self-disclosure, which fosters transference.
  • Client projects feelings from past caregivers; this is material for analysis.

Contemporary view

  • Rejects full neutrality and anonymity.
  • Emphasizes therapeutic alliance and repairing ruptures.
  • Uses empathic attunement to understand the inner world.
  • Help clients gain freedom to love, work, and play; foster self-awareness, honesty, realistic anxiety management, and better relationships.
  • Listen to spoken and unspoken material; attend to resistances, gaps, inconsistencies, dreams, free associations.
  • Use interpretation with tact and timing.
  • Conditions for change: client's readiness matters more than the therapist's accuracy; rushed interpretations fail. Therapy is like assembling puzzle pieces.

Transference, working through, countertransference

Transference
The client's unconscious shifting onto the analyst of feelings, attitudes, and fantasies (positive and negative) that were reactions to significant figures in development.
Working through
Repetitive, elaborate exploration of unconscious material and defenses, most of which originated early.
Countertransference
Inappropriate affect or irrational responses when therapists lose objectivity because their own conflicts are triggered.
Part D · Techniques & critique

Free association

  • Core technique: clients say whatever comes to mind without censorship, even if trivial, illogical, or painful.
  • Opens access to unconscious wishes, conflicts, fantasies, motives; may trigger recollections and catharsis.
  • Resistances and blockages are noted and interpreted as cues to anxiety or conflict.
  • Nothing is taken at face value; spoken and unspoken material matter.

Interpretation

  • The analyst explains meanings of dreams, free association, resistances, defenses, and the therapeutic relationship; helps the ego assimilate new material.
  • Contemporary view: identifying, clarifying, and translating material, shared collaboratively and as hypotheses.
  • Timing rules: interpret only when material is near conscious awareness; start with surface insights before going deeper; poorly timed interpretations get rejected.

Analysis of resistance

  • Anything that hinders progress and blocks unconscious material from awareness: ideas, attitudes, feelings, or actions that maintain the status quo.
  • Freud: resistance is an unconscious defense against anxiety and pain. It often appears in free association or dream analysis.
  • Clients want change yet cling to familiar patterns.
  • Therapist creates a safe climate, points out resistances starting with the most obvious, and does not frame resistance as uncooperativeness. It is both a barrier and a guide to the client's defenses.

Analysis of transference

  • Past relationships shape how clients perceive and relate to the therapist, letting them re-experience unconscious feelings.
  • Interpretation reveals repeated relational patterns in therapy and current life; central technique of classical psychoanalysis.
  • Working through old conflicts within the therapeutic relationship counteracts early relationship effects and promotes awareness and personality change.

Limitations and criticisms

Practical

  • Time, expense, trained therapists needed.
  • Free association, dream analysis, deep transference work often impractical.
  • Severely disturbed clients may lack ego strength.
  • Brief, time-limited psychodynamic therapy emerged in response.

Stance

  • Blank-screen anonymity can be too restrictive; modern approaches use appropriate self-disclosure (Yalom, 2003) and relational analysis.

Feminist critiques

  • Criticism of Oedipus/Electra complexes and heavy emphasis on mother–child bond.
  • Tendency to blame mothers while fathers are absent from the explanations.
  • DBT (Linehan) avoids mother-blaming and recognizes invalidating environments.

Elitism & defense

  • Seen as elitist, suited to highly educated clients.
  • Defenders: an evolving field revised since Freud; adaptations for crisis, trauma, and underserved populations.

Jungian & Adlerian therapy

Individuation and archetypes, then style of life, social interest and courage to change.

Part A · Jungian analytical psychotherapy

Human nature: four stages

Unlike Freud (childhood) and Erikson (lifespan), Jung stressed middle age as the key stage for growth and self-discovery. Personality keeps developing, shaped by culture and the unconscious.

StageKey points
ChildhoodGoverned by primitive urges. Parents help the child individuate. Experiences influence but do not fully shape personality (against Freud). Family-systems approach: treat the whole family. Born with an inherited collective unconscious.
AdolescenceIdentity exploration: relationships, career, values. Often confusing and unstable. Personality preferences emerge (introversion vs. extraversion). Parents may project fears or ambitions onto teens.
Middle age“Second half of life,” most significant. Energy turns inward; shift from extraversion to introversion. Task: integrate conscious and unconscious, toward individuation. Interest in midlife crises.
Old ageReflection and reconciliation. Confronting mortality and legacy; meaning eases acceptance of death. Repressed aspects may resurface. Still a time of growth.

Major constructs of the psyche

Personal conscious
Awareness of self and the outer world; current thoughts and easily retrieved memories. The only fully self-revealed part. Developing uniqueness and self-awareness is individuation.
Ego
Center of consciousness and executive organizer. Manages thoughts, feelings, perceptions; decides what is recalled; balances conscious and unconscious. The psyche is self-regulating.
Personal unconscious
Like Freud’s: repressed memories, forgotten experiences, suppressed behaviors. Emotionally charged; surfaces in dreams, fantasies, creative arts.
Collective unconscious
Jung’s most distinctive idea: inherited, universal patterns shared by all humanity, made of archetypes. Like instinct (weaverbirds building nests). Reflects evolutionary heritage, not personal learning.

Complexes

Repressed content plus strong emotion forms a complex: a cluster of emotionally loaded ideas that drives behavior. Father complex: control, protection (hero worship in women). Mother complex: nurturing, self-sacrifice. Others: God, martyr, superiority, Napoleon. Complexes come from personal experience but carry collective elements.

Archetypes

Universal symbolic images inherited from ancestral experience; found in cultures, myths, religions. Dormant until activated by significant events (e.g., love at first sight from the “ideal partner” archetype).

The Shadow

Dark, repressed side; traits one denies or projects onto others. Projection leads to misreading others or acting out under stress.

Anima and animus

Opposite-gender qualities within. Anima: feminine side in men (nurturing, playful). Animus: masculine side in women (assertive, ambitious).

The Self

Central archetype of wholeness and integration of conscious and unconscious. Compared to syzygy (balanced alignment of anima and animus).

Goals and process of change

  • Facilitate individuation; integrate personal conscious and unconscious; live authentically in the “here and now.”
  • Neurosis comes from a split between powerful complexes, often from trauma. Splitting is universal but pathological when it disrupts functioning. Symptoms are chances for growth.
  • Severe cases: “possessed by the complex” when archetypal forces overpower the ego.
Catharsis·Analytical·Insight·Transformation

Four nonsequential phases: catharsis (emotional release), analytical (dreams, transference, word association, personality types), insight (synthesize history to bring the unconscious into awareness), transformational/individuation (integrate insights, reconcile complexes, achieve wholeness).

Dream analysis

Primary route to the unconscious. Clients record dreams immediately on waking. Jung studied series of dreams for recurring themes, plus emotions and cultural context. Dreams can compensate for one-sided conscious attitudes; symbols need the client’s context.

1 Who/what/when/where→2 Complexes and emotions→3 Turning point→4 Conclusion and meaning

Association

Client shares first thoughts about each dream element.

Amplification

Expand images on three levels: personal (memories), cultural (symbols), archetypal (myth, folklore, religion).

Word association test

Developed by Jung and Eugen Bleuler: 100 neutral and charged words. Jung recorded reaction time and resistance. Complex indicators: delayed or unusual responses, perseveration, refusal. It showed complexes are measurable and observable.

Attitudes and psychological types

Two attitudes

Extraversion: energy outward, sociable. Introversion: energy inward, reflective. Neither is superior; a continuum.

Four functions

Rational (judge): thinking, feeling. Irrational (perceive without judging): sensing, intuition. Each person has a dominant attitude and function; others stay undeveloped and surface in dreams.

TypeExtravertIntrovert
ThinkingRule-bound, logical, detached; often scientistsIntellectual, rigid, subjective, aloof (Jung’s own type)
FeelingWarm, social, conformingQuiet, repressed, seems cold
SensingPleasure-seeking, adventurousArtistic, aesthetic
IntuitingInnovative, opportunisticVisionary, eccentric, impractical

Transference and countertransference

  • Transference: client projects thoughts and fears onto the therapist. Countertransference: therapist projects onto the client. Both must be analyzed.
  • Sources include personal experience, dreams, and archetypal projections.
  • Clients keep journals, reflect, and do creative activities. Therapists must resolve their own issues first, and both stay actively engaged.
Part B · Adlerian therapy

Style of life

How a person adapts, solves problems, and pursues goals; reflects a unique striving for perfection or superiority. Formed by ages 4 to 5 and hard to change. Example: a bullied child who learns verbal manipulation may become a persuasive salesperson. Seen in five life tasks: self-development, spiritual development, occupation, society, love.

Social interest (Gemeinschaftsgefühl)

Aptitude→Ability→Secondary dynamic characteristics
  • Innate but shaped by early relationships. The mother–child bond comes first; neglect or narrow focus can lead to dominating, exploiting, or avoiding.
  • Father: models attitudes to family, work, society; detachment or authoritarianism inhibits it. Marital relationship: conflict models poor cooperation; forgiveness teaches empathy.
  • Low social interest: self-centered, poor health. High: well-being and adaptation, even in old age. Adler believed it could help criminal populations.

Inferiority and superiority

Organ inferiority
Weaker organs prompt compensation; later Adler shifted to social inferiority.
Inferiority as motivation
All infants feel inferior, which drives growth and mastery. Only a problem when it becomes a complex.
Inferiority complex
Belief of not being strong enough to solve life’s problems in a socially useful way. Common in neurotic clients.
Superiority complex
Defense that masks inferiority: arrogance, boasting (“I must show that I am somebody”).

Threats to confidence: physical disability, pampering (overindulgent, overdomineering, overpermissive, overprotective; Capron), and neglect. Pampered children lack independence; neglected ones withdraw.

Goals and the therapeutic relationship

  • Psychotherapy when lifestyle must change; counseling when change fits within the existing lifestyle (Dreikurs). Counseling suffices if only one life task is affected (Manaster and Corsini). Success sign: early recollections change over therapy (Mosak). In practice the difference is small; both aim to increase social interest.
  • Build mutual respect and trust; align goals; educate about realistic goals; keep encouraging; make goals explicit.
  • Empathy addresses feelings and beliefs, since beliefs shape feelings (“I must please,” “Nothing ever works”).
Example: Michelle and her boss

Client can’t please the boss. A stronger response names the belief (“if you can’t please, there’s no point in trying”) and shows her agency to change her behavior.

Assessment: family constellation

The family is a microcosm of society where belonging and inferiority feelings first develop. Adlerians favor family dynamics over strict birth order. Therapists gather sibling descriptions, ages and spacing, trait ratings (hardest worker, bossiest), family events, parental values and discipline, and structural changes (divorce, death, remarriage). Data gathering may take 1 to 4 hours. Goal: understand the client’s perception of self and family.

Assessment: early recollections

  • Specific childhood memories that reveal lifestyle and basic beliefs. Accuracy does not matter; perception does. Reports told by others do not count.
  • Adler used 1 to 2; Dreikurs often 10 or more. Ages 4 to 5 are especially useful.
  • Look for dominant themes, role (participant or observer), emotions, and consistency.

Assessment: dreams

Dreams are purposeful, not random; symbols have no fixed meaning. They show whether a person is stuck or moving, change as therapy progresses, and are used with family constellation and early recollections.

Basic mistakes

Early self-defeating beliefs that oppose social interest (avoidance, self-centeredness, power seeking). People guard them with safeguarding mechanisms.

CategoryExample
Overgeneralizations“Everyone should like me.” “I never do anything right.”
False or impossible goals of security“People want to take advantage of me.”
Misperceptions of life’s demands“Life is too hard.”
Minimization or denial of worth“I’m stupid.”
Faulty values“You have to cheat to get ahead.”

Assets, insight, and interpretation

  • Assets (honesty, skills, responsibility) balance discouragement and strengthen motivation in long analyses.
  • Interpretation targets goals and purposes, not labels. Make it tentative: “Is it possible that…”, “I wonder if…”. Avoid “You feel insecure.”

Reorientation techniques

Clients actively change beliefs and behaviors, take risks, and try new patterns (Dreikurs).

TechniqueWhat it does
ImmediacyAddresses what is happening now; points out mismatch between words and nonverbal cues, tentatively.
EncouragementPrimary ingredient; focuses on strengths and solutions; builds willingness to take risks. More than “just try harder.”
Acting as ifBehave as though the desired outcome will succeed, like trying on a new suit; builds self-efficacy.
Catching oneselfNotice unwanted behavior after, then during, then before it happens; leads to an “Aha!” moment.
Creating imagesMentally rehearse success (e.g., assertively asking a roommate to stop smoking).
Spitting in the client’s soupName the purpose behind a behavior so it loses its appeal.
Avoiding the tar babyDon’t get stuck in the client’s sticky self-defeating patterns; don’t react as others do.
Push-button techniqueRecall a pleasant memory, then an unpleasant one, then the pleasant again: we choose feelings by what we think about (Mosak).
Paradoxical intention“Prescribing the symptom”: exaggerate the problem behavior until it loses appeal.
HomeworkSimple tasks between sessions, reviewed weekly; never controlling the client’s life.

Limitations and criticisms

  • Lack of systematization; vague concepts that are hard to research.
  • Poetic and lecture-based rather than scientific.
  • Translation inconsistencies; not scholarly by modern standards.

Existential therapy

Meaning, freedom, responsibility, and facing the givens of existence.

Part A · Roots

Origins of existential therapy

  • Emerged in the 1940s–1950s across Europe, shaped by post–World War II struggles.
  • People faced deep existential crises: isolation, alienation, meaninglessness.
  • Early thinkers stressed the individual's experience of being alone and confronting existential anxiety.
  • European existentialism highlighted human limitations and the tragic dimensions of life (Sharp & Bugental, 2001).

Philosophical influences

Existential psychology drew on philosophy, literature, and religion (19th-century roots). Early existential psychoanalysts: Ludwig Binswanger and Medard Boss. Contributions as identified by Yalom:

Søren Kierkegaard
Creative anxiety, despair, fear and dread, guilt, nothingness.
Friedrich Nietzsche
Death, suicide, the will to power.
Martin Heidegger
Authentic being, caring, death, guilt, individual responsibility, isolation.
Jean-Paul Sartre
Meaninglessness, freedom, responsibility, choice.
Martin Buber
Interpersonal relationships, the I–Thou encounter in therapy, self-transcendence.
Part B · View of human nature

Essence of the existential movement

  • Reacts against therapy as a set of techniques; asks what it means to be human.
  • Emphasizes respect for the person, openness to diverse experiences, and multiple ways of understanding people.
  • Therapy is grounded in authentic human experience, not rigid methods.

Philosophical balance

  • Seeks equilibrium between recognizing human limitations and tragedy and embracing life's possibilities and growth.
  • Helps people face existential dilemmas: isolation, alienation, meaninglessness. Central focus: being alone in the world and the anxiety of isolation.
  • Existential isolation cannot be removed but can be softened through love and authentic connection (Yalom & Josselson, 2019).

Dynamic human existence

Existence is fluid, evolving, and self-creating: we continually re-create ourselves through choices and actions. People are always in transition, responding to tensions, conflicts, and contradictions; being human means searching for meaning through constant questioning.

Six core themes of the human condition

Self-awareness·Freedom & responsibility·Identity & relationships·Meaning·Anxiety·Death & nonbeing
1. Self-awareness
The ability to reflect on and understand one's existence.
2. Freedom and responsibility
The power and duty to make choices and shape one's life.
3. Identity and relationships
Creating meaning through genuine connections with others.
4. Search for meaning, purpose, values, goals
The drive to find direction in life.
5. Anxiety
Inevitable part of living and of awareness of freedom.
6. Awareness of death and nonbeing
Understanding mortality gives life urgency and significance.
Part C · Goals & interventions

Therapeutic goals

Core purposeExistential therapy is an invitation to live more authentically: to recognize how one is not living fully and make conscious choices toward becoming one's true potential self.
  • Goal: authentic living through self-awareness, responsibility, and action aligned with one's values and purpose.
  • May (1981): clients often enter believing they are enslaved by circumstances and that the therapist can free them. Therapy helps them reclaim ownership of their lives, listen to inner wisdom, and acknowledge what they already know about themselves.
  • Ultimate goal: increased awareness. Through awareness, clients discover new possibilities and realize change is possible.

General approach to interventions

Relationship and process over rigid technique. Techniques complement the relationship and are rooted in phenomenological awareness: understanding each client's unique experience with respect and openness.

1. Telling the story

  • Myths are central to existential meaning (May, 1992); they reflect universal experience and cultural responses (Hoffman, 2009).
  • Sharing one's story begins healing and releases emotional burdens.
  • Integrates past, present, and future into a coherent self.

2. Presence

  • The relationship itself is the primary intervention; therapist and client are existential partners in a genuine encounter.
  • Compassion, respect, empathy (Nanda, 2013); acceptance, calmness, mindfulness; open-mindedness (Landridge, 2013).
  • Listen to the feeling tones beneath words (Spillers, 2007).

3. Dream-work

  • Dreams as tools for existential insight, not only access to the unconscious.
  • Explore emotions, fears, hopes, desires; moves clients toward authenticity.

4. Closure

  • Termination is the final confrontation with reality; endings can trigger resistance.
  • Help the client create a meaningful goodbye; a “good parting” honors growth and connection.

Limitations

  • Requires clients to confront fears, anxieties, and responsibilities directly; change is lifelong, which can be hard.
  • Heavy focus on the interpersonal relationship; may feel intrusive to clients who avoid contact or deep emotional connection.
  • No specific techniques or prescriptive solutions; unsettling for clients who prefer structured, directive, problem-focused therapy.
  • Unscripted and individualized, so hard to measure scientifically. Still, existential principles are increasingly integrated into counseling and health practice, with growing outcome, developmental, meta-analysis and case-study research.
