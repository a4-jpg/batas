What is I-O psychology?

Definition, the two-sided coin, the three fields, and the six key areas.

Definition & purpose

Why the field exists

Apart from sleeping, the average person spends more hours per week working than doing any other activity. That much time deeply affects a person's life, positively and negatively, so studying how the working world affects behavior and psychological makeup is a major focus of psychology. This branch is industrial and organizational (I-O) psychology.

Definition

Definition (Spector, 2008; Jex, 2002)I-O psychology is the study of people working and the application of psychological principles to the organizational and work environment. It is concerned with both the science (studying principles) and the application of those principles.

Focus: improving productivity in the workplace and creating the best possible working environment for employees.

The coin with two sides

Industrial vs. organizational

Industrial side

  • Understand behavior to improve organizational efficiency, employee selection, employee training, and job design.
  • Top-down perspective: how can the organization benefit from psychological principles?

Organizational side

  • Understand behavior to enhance employee satisfaction and well-being.
  • Topics: employee attitudes, employee behavior, job stress, supervisory practices (Spector, 2008).
  • Bottom-up perspective: quality and satisfaction of the individuals rather than the organization as a whole.
The two sides togetherThey are not mutually exclusive. Motivation is relevant to the industrial concern of efficiency and performance, and also to the organizational concern with employees' happiness and well-being (Spector, 2008).
Fields & key areas

Three broad fields

I-O psychology divides into industrial, organizational, and human factors; they overlap but each has distinctive features.

1. Industrial psychology
Describing job requirements and assessing individuals' ability to meet them; training, evaluating, and responding to evaluations after hire; must consider legality of discrimination in hiring.
2. Organizational psychology
How relationships among employees affect them and business performance: satisfaction, motivation, commitment; management, leadership, organizational culture, structures, social norms, role expectations; also harassment (including sexual harassment) and workplace violence.
3. Human factors psychology
How workers interact with the tools of work and how to design tools to optimize productivity, safety, and health (fit of desk, chair, computer; complex displays). In Europe: ergonomics.

Where I-O psychologists work

Four main work areas: academia, government, consulting, and business (see Part 3 for settings in detail).

Six key areas (Muchinsky, 2012)

Most I-O psychologists work in one of six major subject areas. Tap a card to focus on it.

Training and development
Determine skills needed for specific jobs; develop and evaluate employee training programs.
Employee selection
Develop selection assessments such as screening tests to see if applicants are qualified.
Ergonomics
Design procedures and equipment to maximize performance and minimize injury.
Performance management
Develop assessments and techniques to determine whether employees are doing their jobs well.
Work life
Improve employee satisfaction and maximize productivity: make jobs more rewarding; improve quality of life at work.
Organizational development
Help improve organizations: increase profits, redesign products, improve organizational structure.

Origins

Early 20th-century founders

I-O psychology began in the early 20th century. Cattell, Münsterberg, and Scott were students of Wilhelm Wundt, the father of experimental psychology. Some had worked in industrial psychology before World War I and also consulted for businesses.

Industrial firstThis early research was what we now call industrial psychology. Organizational psychology developed as an experimental science later in the century (Katzell & Austin, 1992).
World War I

World War I and personnel selection

  • US entry in April 1917 pulled industrial psychologists into the war effort. Yerkes was president of the 25-year-old APA and organized a group under the Surgeon General's Office to screen and select enlisted men.
  • Army Alpha measured mental abilities; Army Beta was a non-verbal form for illiterate and non-English-speaking draftees.
  • Scott and Bingham organized a group under the Adjutant General's Office to develop officer selection methods: a catalogue of occupational needs (a job-description system), performance ratings, and occupational skill tests.
  • The APA is the US professional association for clinical and research psychologists: conferences, accrediting degree programs, publishing journals.

After the war: Millicent Pond

Pond (PhD, Yale) was director of employment test research at Scoville Manufacturing Company. She compared pre-employment tests with indicators of job performance for factory workers; the studies appeared in the Journal of Personnel Research in the late 1920s.

Hawthorne & social psychology

The Hawthorne studies (1929–1932)

  • Elton Mayo (1880–1949) and colleagues studied Western Electric's Hawthorne Works near Chicago, taking industrial psychology beyond selection and placement to interpersonal relations, motivation, and organizational dynamics. These studies mark the origin of organizational psychology.
  • They began as research on the physical environment (e.g., lighting), but psychological and social factors proved more interesting, including how supervisory style raised or lowered productivity.
Hawthorne effectThe increase in performance of individuals who are noticed, watched, and paid attention to by researchers or supervisors. Any change in a variable (even a return to poor lighting) improved productivity; the effect faded when attention faded (Roethlisberger & Dickson, 1939). It remains an important experimental consideration that must be controlled for.

Kurt Lewin and group dynamics

In the 1930s researchers began studying employees' feelings about their jobs. Kurt Lewin, the founder of social psychology, researched leadership styles, team structure, and team dynamics, including an early study using children to examine leadership style and aggression, group dynamics, and satisfaction. He coined the term “group dynamics.”

Human factors: Taylor & Gilbreth

Frederick Taylor: scientific management

  • An engineer who believed redesigning the workplace would raise both output and wages. Book: The Principles of Scientific Management (1911); used time and motion studies; covered management styles, selection, training, and the work itself.
  • Iron ingots example: work rests raised output from 12.5 to 47.0 tons per day with less fatigue and higher wages (paid by the ton); company cost fell from 9.2 to 3.9 cents per ton.
  • Criticism: feared exploitation and fewer jobs; assumed only managers could find the most efficient method and that workers were fundamentally lazy; maximized productivity with little concern for well-being; criticized by unions.

Lillian Gilbreth: mother of modern management

  • With husband Frank, used time and motion studies to reduce motions per task; applied them to industry, home, office, and shops; studied fatigue and time-management stress; found many employees motivated by money and job satisfaction.
  • Book (1914): The Psychology of Management. Credited with shelves inside refrigerator doors and the foot-pedal trash can. First woman to join the American Society of Mechanical Engineers (1926); Hoover Medal (1966).
ErgonomicsTaylor's and Gilbreth's work improved productivity and the fit between technology and the person using it. The study of machine–human fit is ergonomics or human factors psychology.
World War II and today

World War II and the APA's response

  • Bingham became chief psychologist for the War Department, developing systems for job selection, classification, training, performance review, team development, morale change, and attitude change. Canada and the UK also grew their I-O psychology.
  • After the war both industrial and organizational psychology became major research areas; concerns about the fairness of employment tests arose, and ethnic and gender bias in tests was evaluated; job satisfaction and motivation research grew.
  • Before WWII the APA was not concerned with experimental or applied fields. It created Division 14, Industrial and Business Psychology, which eventually became the Society for Industrial and Organizational Psychology (SIOP).

I-O psychology today

  • SIOP, a division of the APA, listed 8,000 members (2014).
  • The Bureau of Labor Statistics (2013) projected this profession would have the greatest growth of all job classifications in the 20 years after 2012.
  • Average pay: over $80,000 with a master's and over $110,000 with a doctorate (Khanna, Medsker, & Ginter, 2012).

Careers, employers & education

Where I-O psychologists work, what they do, and the degrees that get them there.

Employment

Four employment settings

Colleges and universities

Teach and conduct research; some become administrators (deans, provosts, vice presidents).

Consulting firms

Help many organizations become more productive: select a high-quality, diverse workforce; design fair motivating systems; train employees; ensure legal and ethical treatment of applicants and employees. Sizes range from one-person firms to hundreds of consultants; some specialize (selection, diversity, attitude surveys), others offer a range.

Private sector

Work for a single company such as IBM, Microsoft, or FedEx; consultants work with many companies.

Public sector

Work for local, state, or federal government.

Private sector often pays more, but many believe the job stability of the public sector offsets lower pay. Ph.D.s are far more likely to work in academia; master's graduates more often work as HR generalists, data analysts, trainers, and compensation analysts.

Education

Master's vs. doctoral programs

Master's

  • 1 to 2 years after the bachelor's; typical prerequisites: GPA 3.0, GRE 1,000.
  • About 40 hours of coursework; full graduate load is 9–12 hours (vs. 15–18 undergraduate).
  • Thesis (second year), internship or practicum, and a comprehensive oral/written exam.
  • Two kinds: part of a Ph.D. program, or terminal (no Ph.D. offered): best for applied HR positions; usually easier entry, more aid and individual attention.

Doctoral (Ph.D.)

  • Typically 5 years; common entrance: GPA 3.5, GRE 1,200.
  • First two years: broad psychology courses; I-O courses in years 3 and 4.
  • Thesis plus dissertation and more extensive comprehensive exams.
  • Well-known faculty, better facilities and research funding; best for those who want to teach, research, or consult.
Thesis vs. dissertationNo formal definition, but a dissertation is broader in scope, longer, and requires more original and independent effort (Riddle & Foster, 1997). Information on programs is available from SIOP (siop.org).

Research in I-O psychology

Why research matters and the six considerations in conducting a study.

Why research?

Why conduct research

  • I-O psychology relies heavily on research and statistics. Most important reason: research ultimately saves organizations money (through satisfaction, productivity, fewer accidents). I-O psychologists must save more than they cost.
  • Example: organizations long relied on the employment interview, but research shows the unstructured interview is not the best predictor of future job behavior (Schmidt & Hunter).
  • Research confronts us daily (polls, ads: “nine out of ten dentists”); understanding it helps you judge claims.

Common sense is often wrong

Examples: the world was “flat”; women employees “could not perform as well as men.” Answer-changing: 33 studies show answers are more often changed from wrong to right (Kruger, Wirtz, & Miller, 2005), contrary to “stick with your first answer.”

Six considerations in conducting research
1 Idea / hypothesis / theory→2 Literature review→3 Location→4 Method→5 Sample→6 Run & analyze

1 · Ideas, hypotheses, and theories

  • Many ideas start with “I wonder…”; everyone is a researcher at heart, and research beats guesswork.
  • A hypothesis is an educated prediction about the answer to a question, based on theory, previous research, or logic.

Idea or question

Does all this noise affect my employees' performance?

Hypothesis (what will happen)

High noise will increase errors in assembling electronic components.

Theory (why)

Noise is a distraction, making it hard to concentrate.

Competing theories: the referral example

An I-O psychologist predicts employee referrals yield longer tenure. Four possible explanations:

Realistic job preview theory
Referred applicants get an accurate picture of the job and company. Implication: give every applicant a realistic job preview.
Differential recruitment-source theory
Personalities of applicants differ by the method they use to find jobs.
Personality similarity theory
Friends have similar personalities, so a friend of a stayer will like the job too. Implication: encourage successful employees to recruit friends.
Socialization theory
People who know someone inside are absorbed into the informal system faster, are coached, and have social needs met.

Even when a study supports a hypothesis, it is important to find why; the best theory lets us design better methods.

No hypothesis possible

Little prior research or theory (e.g., personality vs. handwriting neatness): an exploratory study without a hypothesis; common but generally frowned on.

Prediction could go either way

E.g., would a reference letter from a senator be more or less influential than a professor's?

Hypothesis not supported

Often a poor research design or a topic more complicated than thought; e.g., no single theory explains aggression; each is partly true.

2 · Literature reviews

  • Search for similar research first; don't “reinvent the wheel.” Related research gives ideas for your study.
  • Common methods: subject indexes (PsycINFO, Infotrac), browsing journals, searching reference sections, asking researchers.

3 · Location: laboratory vs. field

Laboratory

  • About 32% of I-O research (Roch, 2008); mostly at universities, but also AT&T, OPM, Microsoft.
  • Strength: control of extraneous variables (internal validity).
  • Weakness: external validity (generalizing to the real world).

Field

  • Assembly lines, insurance offices, interview rooms.
  • Gains external validity.
  • Loses control of extraneous variables (internal validity).

4 · Research methods

Choices: experiments, quasi-experiments, archival research, observations, surveys, and meta-analyses.

Experiment
The most powerful method; the only one that can determine cause and effect. Two defining characteristics: (1) manipulation of one or more independent variables and (2) random assignment to experimental and control conditions. Missing either makes it a quasi-experiment, study, survey, or investigation.
Quasi-experiment
Used when an experiment is not possible (e.g., compare average productivity in 100 plants with lower vs. higher noise). Weaker: many other variables could explain differences.
Archival research
Uses previously collected data or records (e.g., personnel files of good vs. poor workers).
Survey
Asks people's opinions (attitudes, recruitment preferences). By mail, interview, phone, fax, email, internet. Choice depends on sample size, budget, time, need for a representative sample.
Meta-analysis
Statistical method combining previous research: find the effect size in each article, then average to a mean effect size.

5 · Subject samples

  • Decide the sample's size, composition, and selection method. A large sample is unnecessary if you can use a random sample and control extraneous variables. A properly conducted survey needs only about 1,000 participants to generalize to the entire US population (Deane, 1999).
  • A small organization may use all employees (small but highly representative); a large one picks certain employees for economic and practical reasons.

6 · Running the study and statistical analysis

  • Running: give instructions in a standardized, understandable way to avoid bias; afterward debrief participants (explain the purpose, allow questions).
  • Statistics: show how confident we are that results are real and did not occur by chance alone.

Ethics in I-O psychology

Ethical dilemmas, Type A vs. Type B, and why people rationalize.

Dilemmas

What is an ethical dilemma?

Organizations and employees face ethical dilemmas daily: ambiguous situations requiring a personal judgment of right or wrong because no rules, policies, or laws guide the decision. People rely on morals and personal values, so different people decide differently in similar situations.

Example: selling a carYou know it has been in several accidents. No law or policy says you must tell the buyer; most would say not to bring it up unless asked. Is that morally wrong? It depends on one's value system.

Type A vs. Type B dilemmas

Type A

  • High uncertainty about what is right or wrong.
  • No clearly best solution; both positive and negative consequences.
  • Example: animal testing of new drugs: hurting living creatures vs. possibly saving millions of lives.

Type B (rationalizing)

  • The difference between right and wrong is clear.
  • People know what is right but choose what benefits themselves.
  • They rationalize: “everyone else does it.”
  • Examples: hiding the car's damage; cheating on a test “just this once.”

Unethical behavior at work

  • The ability to rationalize is why unethical behavior is at an all-time high in organizations.
  • CareerBuilder.com (2005): of 2,050 workers, 19% reported lying in the workplace at some point.
  • Ethics Resource Center (2007): 56% of workers witnessed unethical behavior: taking supplies home, using sick leave when not sick, abuse of employees by management, coming in late and leaving early.
