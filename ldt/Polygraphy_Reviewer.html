Basics & theory

Definitions, stimulus–response, objectives, limits

Key definitions

PolygraphyThe scientific detection of deception through the use or aid of a polygraph. Polygraph = Greek poly (many) + graphos (writings): an instrument recording changes in blood pressure/pulse, respiration and galvanic skin reflex (GSR) as signs of emotional excitement, especially lying.
Lie detection
Recording a person’s psycho-physiological reactions while questioned, with scientific interpretation by trained experts.
Psychophysiology
Study of the nervous system and bodily reactions influenced by mental responses to a stimulus.
Deception / detection
Deceiving or misleading by lying / discovering something hidden.
Lying
Creating a false or misleading impression to wrongfully affect another’s acts, opinion or affection; by words, or by acts, feigns, ruses, marks and symbols.
Emotion / fear / heredity
Acute disturbance of the whole individual / response to danger beyond one’s defensive power / transmission of traits from parents to offspring.
Normal tracing
Tracing produced when an irrelevant question is asked.
Dicrotic notch
Short horizontal line on the cardio tracing in the middle of the diastolic stem.
Chart / polygram
Composite record of pneumograph, galvanograph and cardiosphygmograph tracings from one series of questions.

Stimulus–response theory

TermMeaning
StimulusForce from the environment that excites receptors (a question acting as a stressor)
ReactionActivity aroused by a stimulus; mental, psychological
ResponseMuscular or glandular reaction; physiological (faster breathing, palpitation)
Specific responseResponse to a particular question that deviates from the norm (sweating, nervousness)
BaselineEstablished normal level; changes from it are presumed to show emotional reaction when lying

Examination basics

  • Subject: suspect, complainant, witness, victim, relative, or job applicant. Examiner/polygraphist: conducts the test.
  • Admission: self-incriminating statement short of guilt. Confession: voluntary acknowledgement of guilt with circumstances. The ultimate objective is to obtain either.
  • Questions are answerable by yes or no. Accuracy relies on the competency of the examiner. Cardinal rule: objectivity over subjectivity.
  • Theory: when a mentally normal person lies, recordable physiological changes occur.
  • Uses: criminal investigation, pre-employment screening, periodic integrity checks, claim verification, loyalty test, promotion.
  • In investigation: eliminate innocent subjects, recognize fabricated complaints, determine veracity, narrow inquiry, locate fruits of the crime, gather information from reluctant witnesses.
  • Limitations: only for a willing subject; result is not accepted as court evidence; interpretation may vary.

Methods & ordeals

Ancient ordeals, observation, drugs, hypnotism, word association

Trial by ordeal

Ordeal (Latin dei indicum, “miraculous decisions”): the accused is exposed to physical danger supposedly harmless if innocent. It rested on religious and superstitious belief, not psychology, and lasted to the late 16th century.

OrdealHow it worked
Red hot ironTongue to hot iron nine times (Rajmal, North Bengal); burned means death
BalanceAccuser and accused on a scale (India); the lighter is acquitted
Boiling waterArm into the pot to the elbow (Africa); blisters mark the thief
Rice chewing“Sathee” rice (India); blood in saliva, swollen mouth corner or trembling means liar
Red waterEastern Africa; fast 12 hours, swallow rice and emetic; coughing it up means guilty
CombatLoser is guilty; only legal ordeal in England under Henry III; champions could be hired (Ivanhoe)
CorsnedSwallow a morsel of bread; choking means guilt

By countryBurma: candles, the first to burn out is the liar. Borneo: shellfish, first to move wins. Greece: spinning axe. Nigeria: cock’s feather through the tongue. Madagascar: “tangena” poison fruit juice

Observation and police methods

  • Observation: facial expressions, postural reactions, stimulus–reaction. Signs of emotional tension: color change, sweating, avoiding eye contact, heart rate change, holding breath, dry mouth and throat, unusual behavior.
  • Internal guilt response: quickening palpitation, gripping intestine, dry mouth, sinking stomach, urge to swallow.
  • External guilt response: uneasy hands and feet, avoiding eye contact, monotone voice, fidgeting, Adam’s apple movement; eyes dilated, blushing or paling, lip twitching, excessive winking.
  • 5W1H: who, what, where, when, why, how. 3 I’s: information, interview/interrogation, instrumentation.

Other deception detection methods

Truth serum
Dr. Edward Mandel House. A misnomer: a drug (hyoscine hydrobromide) given until delirium. Narcoanalysis uses sodium amytal or pentothal, which depress the brain’s inhibitory mechanism.
Why not advised
Dangerous, unreliable, removes free will, inadmissible in court (though useful for further investigation).
Intoxication
Alcohol “inhibits the inhibitor”; in vino veritas. Inadmissible unless the subject later recalls and confirms the statement.
Stimulus association
Stimulus words, objects or persons; time interval and reaction are studied.
Hypnotism (1778)
Franz Anton Mesmer, animal magnetism. Needs the subject’s cooperation; inadmissible; strong-willed professionals resist.
Word association
Sir Francis Galton (1879); Carl Jung (Swiss, 1875–1961) developed it. The key factor is response time.
Psychological Stress Evaluator
Displays inaudible voice micro-tremors (8–14 cycles/sec).

Psychology of lying

Why people lie, kinds and types of lies, types of liars

Why lying shows in the body

Lying is an emotional experience. Fear of detection and being penalized triggers automatic, self-regulating changes beyond conscious control, which the polygraph records. Reasons for lying: avoid trouble with the law, avoid hurting feelings, personal gain, avoid embarrassment, a joke.

Admits it but downplays (“only slapped” after a broken nose).
Exaggeration
Inflates for advantage, e.g., on resumes; found via inconsistencies.

History of polygraphy

Pioneers and the Philippine experience

Pioneers

Who / whenContribution

Angelo Mosso (1895)Sphygmomanometer and scientific cradle; fear as sign of deception

Harold Burtt (1918)Confirmed Benussi; blood pressure more diagnostic than respiration
John A. Larson (1921)Continuous BP, pulse, respiration recording (“Bread Lie Detector”); Father of Scientific Lie Detection
William M. Marston“Father of Modern Polygraphy”; systolic BP test, galvanometer; creator of Wonder Woman

John E. Reid (1945)Reid polygraph; control question technique; muscular resistance
Cleve BacksterNumerical scoring; standardized quantitative technique; SKY questions
Philippines: introduced in 1945 by the Military Police Crime Laboratory (Jose Navarro, Conrado Dumlao trained in the U.S.). In 1950 the NBI sent Agustin Patricio to the Keeler Polygraph Institute; Atty. Manuel Roura succeeded him as chief of the NBI Polygraph Division.

The instrument

Pneumograph, cardiosphygmograph, galvanograph, keymograph

Major components

ComponentMeasures / placement
PneumographRespiration. Corrugated rubber tube (about 7–10 inches) around chest and abdomen; two top upper pens (thoracic and abdominal); beaded chain locks the tube
CardiosphygmographBlood pressure and pulse. Cuff on the upper right arm over the brachial artery; bottom (fourth) pen; inflated by hand pump to about 60–80 mm Hg. Parts: sphygmomanometer, pump bulb, resonance control, centering control, vent, 5-inch pen
GalvanographSkin resistance (GSR/electrodermal). Electrodes on left index and ring fingers; longest, third pen; amplifier, 7-inch pen
KeymographChart-drive unit; synchronous motor moves paper 6 to 12 inches per minute
  • Blood pressure terms: systolic = upward, heart contraction; diastolic = downward, heart relaxed; dicrotic notch on the diastolic stem.
  • Chart paper: about 100 ft rolls; heavy vertical lines every ½ inch = 5 seconds; pulse rate per minute = beats in one division × 12. Parts: rubber roller, paper rail guide, cutter bar, pen table, synchronous motor.
  • Remember: the last to attach is the first to detach. The instrumental test runs 5 to 8 minutes.
  • Models: Lafayette conventional (5 pens), Stoelting conventional (4 pens), LX 4000 computerized, handheld.

Examination procedure

Request, four phases, pre-test conditions, room, chart marking

Requests and phases

  • Requires a formal written request (PNP, AFP, other agencies, court order, or a private individual approved by the Director, PNPCL), hand-carried by the investigator-on-case with the investigation report, sworn statements, crime scene sketch and background data. Telephone requests only in emergencies; radio requests state the case, number of subjects and location.
  • Only for offenses under the RPC, Articles of War or special laws; civil cases only by court or higher-headquarters order. Cases already examined by another agency are not accepted unless directed.
PhaseWhat happens
1 Initial interviewWith the investigator: obtain and evaluate case facts, decide areas to ask about
2 Pre-test interviewWith the subject, 20–30 minutes: constitutional rights (silence, informed of accusation, counsel, against self-incrimination), written consent, personal data, suitability, observation, psychological preparation
3 Instrumental testAttach transducers, give instructions, stimulate and condition, preliminary evaluation
4 Post-test interviewIf deception is indicated: short interrogation for admission or confession; if truthful: released cordially

Conditions and room

  • No medication 2 days before; not hungry; no smoking 2 hours before; no alcohol 12 hours before; at least 5 hours of sleep; calm; no tight clothing; no abuse.
  • A pregnant or menstruating female subject is not tested. Postpone for insufficient sleep or temporary illness.
  • Room: quiet, private, semi-soundproof, plain and ventilated, with an adjoining observation room with a one-way mirror.

Charts, questions & tests

Interpretation rules, question types, test types, court

Interpretation rules

  • General rule: there must be a specific response; it must deviate from the norm; it must appear in at least two charts; the best sign of deception is a specific response in all three tracings at once.
  • Legal rule: specific responses appear consistently on relevant questions in two or more charts.
  • Deception signs: galvanograph (double saddle, rise from baseline, vertical rise, long duration, plunging); cardio (slow pulse, roller-coaster shape); respiration (blocking, suppression, staircase suppression, baseline rise).

Question formulation and types

Questions must be simple, direct, in language the subject understands, answerable by yes or no, about one offense only; no legal terms (rape, murder), accusations, inferences or references to religion or race.

TypePurpose
IrrelevantNo bearing on the case; establishes the norm and lowers excitement
RelevantKey questions to detect deception; strong (primary) vs. weak (secondary, guilty knowledge)
Sacrifice (DYAT)Absorbs the response to the first relevant question (“Do you intend to answer truthfully?”)
Knowledge (DYK)Does the subject know the offender or evidence location
Evidence connectingStimulates a guilty subject with evidence found at the scene
Control (probable lie)Gets a response from the innocent; primary (known lie, 3–5 years before) or secondary (up to the present)
SymptomaticDetects an outside issue worrying the subject
SKY (Backster)Suspect, Know, You; “Did you steal…?”
Check / fishing expeditionLast lie-test question about truthfulness / routine questions to vagrants with no known offense

Types of tests

General question test (GQT)
Planned sequence of irrelevant, relevant and control questions to compare responses; at least five tests when deception is indicated.
Card test
Subject picks one of seven numbered cards and says “No” to all; used to find the lie and stimulate lying subjects.
Peak of tension
Only one question relates to the case; used when only the offender knows the details.
Silent answer
Confirmatory; the subject answers silently.
Guilt complex
Fictitious similar incident for an overly responsive subject; diagnostic for post-test interrogation.
Mixed question
Rearranged first and third test questions to rule out accidental responses.

Physiology, admissibility, accuracy

  • The hypothalamus triggers the physiological changes (fight-or-flight): higher pulse and blood pressure.
  • The result is not conclusive (it is an opinion). It can be admitted by stipulation, by court order, or when the defense shows the examiner tested the accused at the investigator’s request and offers the report.
  • Accuracy factors: the instrument, the subject’s condition, the room, the examiner’s qualifications. With a trained examiner and established procedure, accuracy is generally 85–95% for specific-issue investigations.
