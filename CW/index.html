Foundations of creative writing

What it is, and how it differs from technical and academic writing.

Five basic types of writing

Technical
Specific information about a technical subject, for a specific audience and purpose.
Creative
Fiction, poetry, short stories, plays, novels. Most different from technical.
Expressive
Subjective response to personal experience (journals, diaries).
Expository
“Exposes” a topic analytically and objectively (news reports). Explains without demanding action.
Persuasive
Depends on emotional appeal; aims to change attitudes or motivate action.

Technical vs. creative writing

TechnicalCreative
ContentFactual, straightforwardImaginative, metaphoric or symbolic
AudienceSpecificGeneral
PurposeInform, instruct, persuadeEntertain, provoke, captivate
StyleFormal, standard, academicInformal, artistic, figurative
ToneObjectiveSubjective
VocabularySpecializedGeneral, evocative
OrganizationSequential, systematicArbitrary, artistic

Creative work is mostly self-created, while technical work relies on established facts. Humor and satire fit creative writing but not technical writing.

Imaginative vs. academic writing

Academic

Rigid, procedural, structured by strict guidelines. Conveys knowledge, data, information. Needs factual evidence. Everyone should master it.

Creative

Inspired and artistic; uses word pictures, concepts, deep meaning. Allows more personal expression and no fixed style.

Principal differenceStyle is the chief difference between academic and creative writing.

What is creative writing?

“The art of making things up”: original, self-expressive writing that is not academic or technical but still attracts an audience. Its purpose is to entertain and share human experience (love, loss), reaching for truth about humanity through poetics and storytelling. The first step is to use your imagination.

Types

Poetry, plays, movie/TV scripts, fiction (novels, novellas, short stories), songs, speeches, memoirs, personal essays. Memoirs and personal essays are nonfiction but can be creative, especially in first person.

Techniques

Character and plot development, vivid setting, underlying theme, point of view, dialogue, anecdotes, metaphors and similes, figures of speech, imaginative language, emotional appeal, heavy description.

Sensory details

Sight, sound, touch, smell, taste engage readers and make writing universal and intimate. No one sense matters most; it depends on the scene, though imagery (sight) is common. Adjectives help set mood and tone and build voice.

Example: before and after

Flat: “I went to the store and bought some flowers.” Vivid: yellow tulips in a rusty cart, minty dried eucalyptus, the stench of seafood. The details pull the reader into the scene.

Imagery, symbolism, and diction

The language tools writers use to create pictures and feeling.

ImageryLanguage that uses words or figures of speech to create a vivid mental picture or physical sensation. It is not always visual: it can appeal to any of the five senses.
TypeDescribesIncludes
VisualWhat we seeColor, shape, size, pattern
AuditoryWhat we hearMusic, noise, silence
TactileWhat we touch or feelTemperature, texture, touch, movement
OlfactoryWhat we smellFragrances, odors
GustatoryWhat we tasteSweet, sour, salty, spicy, savory

Why use it: we experience life through the senses, so imagery lets readers sympathize with characters and builds poetry, narratives, plays, film sets, and songs.

Symbolism

Carl Jung: a symbol is a term, name, or picture familiar in daily life that carries specific connotations beyond its obvious meaning. Symbols can be cultural or religious (a country’s flag, the cross).

Diction

Word choice: analyzing how a writer uses language for a distinct purpose and effect.

Informal vs. formal

Informal (personal writing): bug, folks, job, kid, boss. Formal (academic or literary): germ, relatives, position, child, superior.

Types

Colloquial: conversational, maybe dialect. Slang: highly informal. Jargon: special language of a profession or group.

Reading and writing fiction

A series of imagined facts that illustrates truths about human life.

Fiction need not present actual people, though characters and events may be based on real ones. It is not opposed to truth: its situations illustrate what may and can happen.

Principal types

Short story

Brief artistic prose centered on a single main incident, aiming at one dominant impression. Marked by economy, compression, emphasis.

Novel

Book-length prose, usually 75,000 to 100,000 words. Room for more characters, a complicated plot, elaborate settings, more themes.

Character·Point of view·Plot·Setting & atmosphere·Conflict·Irony·Theme

These are the seven elements of the genre.

1. Character

An imagined person in a story (not limited to humans), sometimes modeled on real people. The first essential ingredient of a successful story.

Stock / stereotyped
Needs less-detailed portrayal.
Hero / heroine
Good lead who opposes the villain. In modern fiction the lead is often an ordinary person. An antihero is flawed and does not fit the heroic mold.
Protagonist / antagonist
Neutral, older word for the lead (no implied virtue); the opponent is the antagonist.
Major vs. minor
Lead characters are more complex than the other figures.
Foil
Contrasts with the major character to highlight that character’s qualities.
Flat vs. round
Flat: stock, barest description, advances plot. Round: usually the protagonist; complex, more than one trait.
Static vs. dynamic
Static: no basic change. Dynamic: changes through the story; sudden changes should still be expected from events.
Writing tips: creating characters

Egri’s three dimensions: physical (body type, health, clothing, movement), sociological (name, status, race, family, residence, education, profession, beliefs), psychological (personality, speech, attitudes, habits, dreams, fears). Also: give the character a past; imagine them as real people with emotions and contradictions; show them in action.

2. Point of view

The narrator’s vantage point: focus works like a camera (the frame for characters and events); voice is the words that embody the story.

PersonPronounsEffect
FirstI, weNarrator participates; limited to the narrator’s knowledge
SecondYouAddresses another character; reader feels like a character; conversational
Thirdhe, she, theyMost common; non-participant narrator; “I/you/me” only in dialogue

All-knowing (omniscient)

Sees into every character’s mind, past and future. Editorial omniscience: occasional author opinions. Authorial intrusion: direct comment on an action that readers may find meddlesome.

Limited (selective omniscience / central intelligence)

Term from Henry James. One major or minor character is the sole viewpoint; a non-participating witness.

Devices: stream of consciousness renders the unordered flow of impressions in the mind; interior monologue lets a character speak thoughts aloud for readers to overhear.

3. Plot

A sequence of events with a beginning, middle, and end; the story’s skeleton.

  • Exposition: introduces characters, situation, time, place. You may begin in medias res (in the middle of things).
  • Rising action: conflict and complications, external or internal.
  • Climax: point of greatest tension; starts the falling action.
  • Resolution / denouement: “untying of a knot”; return to stability; a moment of insight. Closed ties up everything (like mysteries); open leaves thought-provoking loose ends.
  • Simple stories have one plot; complex ones over longer time are modular or episodic.

4. Setting and atmosphere

Setting: place and time (place is also called locale). Atmosphere / mood: evokes feelings in readers. Environments: physical (discernible things), sociological (cultural, economic, political), psychological (the place’s personality).

5. Conflict

An event or situation that shakes a stable situation; a struggle between opposing forces.

KindTypeMeaning
ExternalMan vs. natureAgainst an animal or force of nature
ExternalMan vs. manCharacters pitted against each other
ExternalMan vs. societyAgainst man-made institutions
InternalMan vs. selfOvercoming one’s nature or choosing between paths

6. Irony

Words used to mean the opposite, or a gap between expectation and reality.

Verbal
A character says what they do not mean.
Situational
Outcome contradicts expectation (laughing at another’s misfortune that then befalls you).
Dramatic
Readers know what characters do not (Romeo and Juliet).

7. Theme

The central idea, thesis, or message, or an abstraction from it. To find it, look at: dramatic issue (the core premise, revolving around human needs), moral (an objective universal truth the story shows), and insight (deep, intuitive understanding of a person or thing).

Narrative devicesForeshadowing: a hint of what will happen later. Symbol: a concrete thing standing for an abstraction. Mood is the author’s attitude toward the subject; tone is the attitude toward the audience.

Reading and writing poetry

Rhythm, sound, image, and density: from haiku to free verse.

Poetry vs. verse

Poetry: rhythmic expression of intense perceptions, often reserved for verse of high merit. Verse: the metrical line as the basic unit, or any metrical composition; does not indicate quality.

Poetry vs. prose (Stephen Minot)

  1. Poetic line, not sentence, as primary unit
  2. Images over abstractions
  3. Cultivates sound of words
  4. Develops rhythms
  5. Density: implies far more than stated

Essential elements

Theme
Central idea or message.
Tone
Poet’s attitude toward the audience (serious, humorous, formal, intimate, ironic, etc.).
Mood
Poet’s attitude toward the subject matter.
Atmosphere
Dominant emotional aura at any point; appeals to the five senses.
Persona
Speaking voice of the poem; from the Greek mask worn by actors (dramatis personae).

Rhyme

By position

End: line endings. Internal: within a line or across lines. Leonine: last stressed syllable before the caesura rhymes with the line’s last. Beginning: first syllables of lines.

Rhythm and meter

Rhythm: alternation of stressed and unstressed syllables (Greek rhythmos, “measured motion”). Meter: regular recurrence of rhythmic patterns.

4 types of meter

  1. Quantitative: long and short syllables
  2. Accentual (sprung): strong stresses, any number of unstressed
  3. Syllabic: fixed syllable count
  4. Accentual-syllabic: fixed accents and syllables

Nonconventional forms

  • Free verse (vers libre): frees poetry from strict rhyme and meter; rhythm comes from the rise and fall of the whole poem. Early practitioners: Eliot, Emerson, Hulme, Pound, Whitman (first widely acclaimed American free-verse poet).
  • Enjambment: thought spills over from one line to the next without pause or syntactic break, usually without punctuation.
  • Lineation: line length and breaks shape the poem’s silhouette: long lines thick, short lines thin.
  • Typography: style and arrangement of printed letters on the page.
  • Prose poetry: hybrid; sentences and paragraphs, but with rhythm, figures of speech, voice, tone, mood.
  • Concrete poetry (shaped, visual, pattern): shape of words and letters as an image, as in George Herbert’s “Easter Wings.”
  • Performance poetry (spoken word, poetry slam): theatrical stage as page; live encounter builds community.
Writing tips for poets

Avoid verbosity in Japanese-inspired forms. A haiku needs a concrete environmental detail, a season or time-of-day allusion, and a subtle reflection on nature. A tanka’s upper phrase holds a powerful image; the lower, a poignant insight. For fixed forms, keep a list of rhyming words. An ode uses lofty words and elevated tone; an elegy uses solemn words and melancholic tone. End lines on heavy parts of speech (verbs, nouns, pronouns), occasionally on modifiers, and avoid ending on articles, prepositions, or conjunctions unless for emphasis. The end of a line is a one-beat pause; the end of a stanza a two-beat pause.
