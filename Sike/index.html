Assessment for Education

Intervention, Tests, Checklists — Presented 2025-11-21

01

The Case For & Against Educational Testing

Overview

Moderate Pro-Testing View

The argument against standardized tests as the primary cause of educational failure is countered by a moderate view that sees standardized tools of assessment as a necessary and desirable element of an educational system.

  • Tests used for screening purposes can identify at-risk students.
  • Diagnostic data can pinpoint areas of weakness or strength for individual students.
  • Test data can help educators track progress of single or multiple students, providing insights for remediation or educational interventions.
02

The Response to Intervention (RtI) Model

Specific Learning Disability (SLD)

A disorder that affects one or more basic psychological processes involved in using or understanding language — both spoken and written. Can result in imperfect abilities in listening, thinking, speaking, reading, writing, spelling, or doing mathematical calculations.

Response to Intervention (RtI) Model

A multilevel prevention framework used in educational settings to maximize student achievement. Primary objective: accelerate learning for all students by providing appropriate intervention and instruction tailored to their needs. Also serves as a process to identify students with learning disabilities.

RtI Steps

How RtI Works (5-Step Cycle)

  • 1Teachers provide evidence-based instruction.
  • 2Student learning of that instruction is regularly evaluated.
  • 3Intervention, if required, occurs as an appropriate adjustment in the instruction.
  • 4Reevaluation of learning takes place.
  • 5Intervention and reassessment occur as necessary.
Three Levels

RtI is Multilevel — At Least 3 Levels of Intervention

LEVEL 1
Classroom (Universal)

The classroom environment wherein all students are being taught whatever the teacher is teaching.

LEVEL 2
Small Group

A small group of learners who have failed to make adequate classroom progress are segregated for special teaching.

LEVEL 3
Individual

Individually tailored and administered instruction for students who have failed to respond to Level 2.

Key Terms

Related Assessment Concepts

TermDefinition
Problem-Solving Model Use of interventions tailored to students' individual needs, selected by a multidisciplinary team of school professionals.
General Intervention An intervention selected by the school's administration, designed to address the needs of multiple students.
Integrative Assessment A multidisciplinary approach to evaluation that assimilates input from relevant sources.
Dynamic Assessment Differs from static tests by using a test → intervention → retest model to explore learning potential. Originally developed for children but usable with any age.
03

Achievement Tests

Achievement Tests

Designed to measure accomplishment — the degree of learning that has taken place as a result of exposure to a relatively defined learning experience. Can be standardized or non-standardized. Used to assess student progress, compare accomplishments to peers, and inform instructional strategies. A sound achievement test adequately samples the targeted subject matter and reliably gauges learning.

General Achievement

Measures of General Achievement — Batteries

TestKey Details
Achievement Batteries Cover multiple areas; can be administered individually or in groups; divided into several subtests.
Wide Range Achievement Test-4 (WRAT-4) Measures reading, spelling, arithmetic, and (new in 4th ed.) reading comprehension.
STEP Battery Comprehensive K–12 achievement test. Assesses reading, vocabulary, math, writing, study skills, science, social studies. Also includes a behavior inventory, educational environment questionnaire, and activities inventory. Commonly used to identify gifted children.
WIAT-III 16-subtest battery for individuals aged 4 to 50. Yields data on reading, writing, mathematics, listening, and speaking. Used in school and clinical settings.
School Levels

Achievement in Specific Subject Areas by Level

  • ELEMENTARYFocuses on basic skills (reading, writing, math). Various reading achievement tests available. Most measures are teacher-made tests.
  • SECONDARYCooperative Achievement Test — separate tests in English, math, literature, social studies, science, and foreign languages. May also assess minimum competencies for high school diploma.
  • COLLEGEAssess whether college credit should be awarded for learning inside or outside the classroom. Often take the form of end-of-major outcomes assessments.
CBA & CBM

Curriculum-Based Assessment & Measurement

TermDefinition
CBA (Curriculum-Based Assessment) Assessment of information acquired from teachings at school.
CBM (Curriculum-Based Measurement) A type of CBA that uses standardized procedures to evaluate student performance on curriculum-based tasks.

Achievement test items can be categorized as: [1] Fact-Based Items — rely on rote memory  |  [2] Conceptual Items — require application of knowledge.

04

Aptitude Tests

Aptitude Tests

Also known as prognostic tests. Tend to focus more on informal learning or life experiences rather than structured learning. Used to make predictions about a broad range of information and abilities. Compare: achievement tests assess what was learned; aptitude tests predict future performance.

Preschool Level

Assessment at the Preschool Level

The preschool period covers the first five years of life — a time of profound change when basic reflexes and sensorimotor milestones develop (crawling, walking, grasping).

  • Assessment involves observation of behavior, skills, and development over time.
  • Tracks: language development, fine/gross motor skills, social-emotional development, and cognitive abilities.
  • Screening for developmental delays or disabilities (e.g., autism, hearing loss) enables early intervention.
  • Parental involvement is a key component — parents provide important developmental information.
Checklists & Rating Scales

Tools Used in Assessment

ToolDefinition
Checklist A questionnaire that marks the presence or absence of a specific item.
Rating Scale A form used by an evaluator to make a relative judgment of a specified variable or list of variables. Both can be economical, quick, and assess frequency of behavior.
APGAR Score A rating scale that evaluates newborn infants' health — developed by Virginia Apgar. Measures five variables: Activity, Pulse, Grimace, Appearance, Respiration.
Informal Evaluation A brief, unofficial assessment conducted by anyone for any reason without following professional standards.
Behavior Checklists

Key Behavioral Assessment Tools

CBCL
Achenbach Child Behavior Checklist

For ages 4–18. Covers competence items (activities, social relations, school performance) and behavioral/emotional problems. Completed by those with close relationships to the subject.

CRS-R
Connors Rating Scales — Revised

Used to screen for ADHD and other behavior problems.

BASC-2
Behavior Assessment System for Children-2

Uses teacher and parent ratings to identify adaptive difficulties on 16 scales (ranging from ADLs to study skills).

SRP
Self-Report of Personality

May be administered if respondents are believed to have sufficient insight into their own behavior.

Elementary Level

School Readiness — Elementary School

School readiness tests assess an incoming student's abilities, since children of the same chronological age may vary widely in their abilities.

TestDetails
MRT6 — Metropolitan Readiness Tests, 6th Ed. Untimed battery assessing development of reading and math skills needed for early formal school learning. Orally administered (~90 min). Two levels:
Level I (Individual) — beginning/middle kindergarteners
Level II (Group) — end of kindergarten through 1st grade
Secondary Level

Scholastic Aptitude Test (SAT)

Consists of two parts: (1) A multipart test (reading, writing, and mathematics) and (2) SAT subject tests.

  • READINGMeasured through reading comprehension tasks — short passages followed by sentence completion items.
  • WRITINGTests grammar, usage, and word choice via multiple-choice items and an essay question.
  • MATHProbes algebra, geometry, basic statistics, and probability.
  • SUBJECT TESTSOne-hour tests measuring achievement in specific subject areas (English, History, Math, Science, Languages).

SAT scores combined with high school GPA yield the best available predictor of academic success in college, according to its developers.

ACT Assessment

A curriculum-based test that measures high school subject areas. Predictive of academic success and creativity. The ACT and SAT are highly correlated with each other and with general intelligence. Scores help admissions committees determine candidates likely to succeed and serve a gatekeeping function for institutions.

College Level & Beyond

Graduate & Professional Aptitude Tests

TestKey Details
GRE (Graduate Record Examinations) Entrance exam for graduate school. General Test contains:
Verbal — analyze/evaluate written materials, recognize relationships
Quantitative — basic mathematical concepts, quantitative reasoning
Analytical Writing — critical thinking, articulate and argue ideas effectively
MAT (Miller Analogies Test) 100-item, multiple-choice analogy test. Draws on the examinee's ability to perceive relationships, general intelligence, vocabulary, and academic learning.
MCAT Medical College Admission Test. Four sections: Verbal Reasoning, Physical Sciences, Writing Sample, and Biological Sciences.
Seashore Measures of Musical Talents Classic measure of musical aptitude. Six subtests measuring specific aspects of musical talent.
Horn Art Aptitude Inventory Measures various aspects of artistic aptitude.
05

Diagnostic Tests

Diagnostic Tests

Used to identify specific deficits in a student's knowledge or skills for the purpose of targeted intervention. Typically administered to students who have already shown poor performance in a subject area. Usually contain subtests to analyze different aspects of the subject area being tested — but do not necessarily provide information on the underlying causes of the difficulty.

Evaluative vs. Diagnostic

Two Main Categories of Test Data

TypePurpose
Evaluative Applied to tests used to make judgments — e.g., pass/fail, admit/reject decisions.
Diagnostic Applied to tests used to pinpoint a student's difficulty, usually for remedial purposes.
Reading Tests

Key Diagnostic Reading Tests

STANFORD
Stanford Diagnostic Reading Test

Grades 1–12. Measures vocabulary, comprehension, and word analysis.

METROPOLITAN
Metropolitan Reading Instructional Tests

Assesses early reading skills: readiness, word recognition, and comprehension.

DRS
Diagnostic Reading Scales

Measures phonological awareness, decoding, and reading comprehension.

DURRELL
Durrell Analysis of Reading Test

Grades 2–8. Assesses word recognition, decoding, and comprehension.

WRMT-III

Woodcock Reading Mastery Tests — Revised (WRMT-III)

Paper-and-pencil measure of reading readiness, achievement, and difficulties. Ages 4.5 to 80. Takes 15–45 minutes. Five original subtests + three new in the third edition:

  • 1Letter Identification — naming letters in different forms (cursive/printed, upper/lowercase).
  • 2Word Identification — reading isolated words of increasing difficulty aloud.
  • 3Word Attack — pronouncing nonsense syllables using phonetic and structural analysis.
  • 4Word Comprehension — assessing word meaning using a four-part analogy format.
  • 5Passage Comprehension — supplying a missing word in a phrase, sentence, or short paragraph.
  • NEWPhonological Awareness — new in 3rd edition.
  • NEWListening Comprehension — new in 3rd edition.
  • NEWOral Reading Fluency — new in 3rd edition.
Math Tests

Key Diagnostic Math Tests

TestKey Details
SDMT4 — Stanford Diagnostic Mathematics Test, 4th Ed. Evaluates mathematical knowledge and skill. Subtests assess basic concepts, computation, applications, and problem-solving.
KeyMath 3 Diagnostic System (KeyMath3-DA) Comprehensive, norm-referenced test for ages 4½ to 21. 10 subtests. Measures mathematical knowledge and application, problem solving, and quantitative reasoning. Provides specific recommendations for intervention.
06

Psychoeducational Test Batteries

Psychoeducational Test Batteries

Test kits that contain two types of tests: [1] Those that measure abilities related to academic success and [2] Those that measure educational achievement in areas such as reading and arithmetic. Allow for normative comparisons as well as individual evaluation of strengths and weaknesses.

K-ABC

Kaufman Assessment Battery for Children (K-ABC)

Designed for ages 2½ to 12½. Includes intelligence and achievement subtests.

  • Intelligence subtests divided into: Simultaneous skills and Sequential skills (reflecting Luria's two information-processing skills).
  • A third factor identified as verbal comprehension and reading achievement.
  • A student's processing strength determines the teaching method used.

KABC-II expanded age range to 3–18. 10 new subtests added; 8 existing removed; only 8 original subtests remained. Added grounding in Cattell-Horn-Carroll (CHC) theory alongside Luria's theory.

MODEL 1
CHC Model

For children from a mainstream cultural and language background.

MODEL 2
Luria Model

Used if Crystallized Ability would not be a fair indicator of the child's cognitive ability. Excludes verbal ability.

WJ IV

Woodcock-Johnson IV (WJ IV)

Designed for ages 2 to 90. Based on Cattell-Horn-Carroll (CHC) theory. Consists of three co-normed batteries:

BATTERY 1
Tests of Achievement

Measures academic skills across multiple domains.

BATTERY 2
Tests of Cognitive Ability

Yields GIA, Gf (fluid), Gc (crystallized), and Gf-Gc composite measures.

BATTERY 3
Tests of Oral Language

12 tests total — 9 English language tests + 3 parallel Spanish tests.

Battery TypeBest Used For
Standard Battery Brief screenings, periodic re-evaluations, and relatively pinpointed assessments related to instruction, performance level, or RtI.
Extended Battery Most comprehensive and detailed evaluation of an assessee's strengths and weaknesses or educational progress.

Other Tools of Assessment in Educational Settings

Performance Assessment

Performance, Portfolio & Authentic Assessment

TypeDefinition
Performance Assessment Any assessment that goes beyond multiple-choice and requires the examinee to perform a task or demonstrate a skill. Evaluated by criteria established by experts; focuses on knowledge, skills, and values.
Performance Task A work sample designed to elicit representative knowledge, skills, and values from a particular domain of study.
Portfolio Assessment A form of performance assessment — evaluation of one's work samples or portfolio.
Authentic Assessment Evaluation of relevant, meaningful tasks that demonstrate a student's ability to transfer learning to real-world activities. Performance-based; evaluates mastery of academic subject matter.
Peer Appraisal

Peer Appraisal Techniques

Peer Appraisal — a method of evaluation that involves obtaining feedback from an individual's peers. Useful for identifying individuals experiencing difficulties, and allows a perspective from those alongside the person being evaluated.

TECHNIQUE 1
"Guess Who?" Technique

Descriptive sentences are read or handed to students as questionnaires. Students are asked to guess who they refer to. Used in elementary schools.

TECHNIQUE 2
Nominating Techniques

Involves selecting or nominating individuals for different activities.

VISUAL
Sociogram

Peer appraisal results illustrated using figures representing individuals, with lines/arrows indicating types of interaction.

Study Habits & Attitudes

Measuring Study Habits, Interests & Attitudes

Academic performance depends on ability and motivation. Various assessments measure contributing factors.

ToolDescription
Study Habit Checklist For grades 9–14. 37 items assessing study habits related to note taking, reading material, and general study practices.
What I Like to Do Interest Inventory 150 forced-choice items assessing four interest areas: academic, artistic, occupational, and leisure time activities.
Survey of School Attitudes & Quality of School Life Scales Attitude inventories assessing student attitudes toward a variety of school-related factors. General in scope.
SSHA — Survey of Study Habits and Attitudes Grades 7 through college. 100 items. Two forms: Form H (grades 7–12) and Form C (college) — each ~20–25 min. 5-point scale (rarely → almost always). Six areas: Delay Avoidance, Work Methods, Study Habits, Teacher Approval, Education Acceptance, Study Attitudes. Yields: study skills score, attitude score, and total orientation score.

Personality Assessment

Traits, Types, States & Basic Questions — Presented 2025-11-18

01

Personality & Personality Assessment

Personality

An individual's unique constellation of psychological traits that is relatively stable over time. Examples: sense of humor, how you think and behave, how you see the world. When we talk about personality, we use words like trait, type, or state.

Personality Assessment

A way of measuring and evaluating various psychological characteristics — such as traits, values, interests, and attitudes.

02

Traits, Types, and States

Personality Traits

What Are Traits?

Personality Traits — enduring and distinguishable ways in which individuals differ from one another. Used to identify threads of consistency in behavioral patterns.

  • Tend to remain consistent over time but can change or be expressed differently depending on the situation (relatively enduring).
  • The context in which the behavior is displayed is important (contextual).
  • Different situations can require different trait terms (distinguishable).
  • Trait terms are always relative and depend on both the behavior and the context.
Personality Types

What Are Types?

Groups of personality traits commonly found together. Identified as a specific category of personality, described as a pattern rather than individual characteristics.

TypologyDescription
[1] Carl Jung's Typology Basis for the Myers-Briggs Type Indicator (MBTI). Categorizes people based on preferences in perceiving and judging.
[2] Holland's Self-Directed Search Six personality types: Artistic, Enterprising, Investigative, Social, Realistic, Conventional. Used for vocational guidance.
[3] Type A & Type B (Friedman & Rosenman) Type A: competitive, impatient, time-pressured, achievement-oriented. Type B: more mellow and laid-back. Assessed with the Jenkins Activity Survey (52-item self-report).
[4] MMPI The most studied personality typology. Different profiles of scores are associated with different patterns of behavior. A profile = a description or graph showing how much a person exhibits certain characteristics.
Personality States

What Are States?

Personality State has two meanings in the literature:

  • [1] Psychodynamic: describes the dynamic conflict between id, ego, and superego.
  • [2] More Common: refers to the temporary display of a particular personality trait.

Trait = relatively long-lasting predisposition.  |  State = relatively temporary one. Most personality tests do not differentiate between the two.

State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI)

Created by Charles D. Spielberger and colleagues. Asks people to describe their anxiety as: (1) they feel right now or (2) in general — rating intensity or frequency. Distinguishes between enduring anxiety traits and temporary anxiety states.

03

Personality Assessment: Some Basic Questions

Overview

The Key Questions in Personality Assessment

Personality assessment is not just about why we do it. Important factors that influence results:

  • WHO — who is being assessed, and who is doing the assessment?
  • WHAT — what aspects of personality are being assessed?
  • WHERE — where is the assessment taking place?
  • HOW — how is it being conducted?
WHO

Who Is Assessed? Who Does the Assessing?

Some methods rely on the assessee's self-report; others rely on informants.

SourceDetails
Self-Report Information from the person being assessed — diaries, oral/written questions, or test items. Used for very private information only the person can provide. Often used to understand self-concept.
Self-Concept Beliefs, opinions, and attitudes about oneself. A self-concept measure gathers this info and compares it to how others view themselves on the same factors.
TOOL
Beck Self-Concept Test

Asks respondents to compare themselves to others on various traits.

TOOL
Piers-Harris Self-Concept Scale

For children. 80 self-statements covering 6 areas: (1) behavior, (2) intellectual/school status, (3) physical appearance, (4) anxiety, (5) popularity, (6) happiness and satisfaction.

TOOL
BYI-II — Beck Youth Inventories, 2nd Ed.

Inventories measuring depression, anxiety, anger, and disruptive behavior in children/adolescents aged 7–18. Includes a self-concept measure.

Rater Biases

Considerations When One Person Evaluates Another

Leniency / Severity

Raters vary in how generous or strict they are in their ratings, affecting accuracy.

Error of Central Tendency

A tendency to rate everyone near the midpoint of a rating scale.

Halo Effect

A favorable response bias where positive impressions in one area influence all other ratings.

Context Effects

Different raters may have different perspectives based on the context in which they typically interact with the individual.

WHAT

What Is Assessed? — Content & Response Style

Tests measure both primary content areas (targeted traits or states; behavior in certain contexts) and the test-taker's response style.

Response Style — how a person answers test items or interview questions regardless of the content.

[1] Socially Desirable Responding

Tendency to present oneself in a favorable, socially acceptable light.

[2] Acquiescence

Tendency to agree with whatever is presented.

[3] Nonacquiescence

Tendency to disagree with whatever is presented.

[4] Deviance

Tendency to make unusual or uncommon responses.

[5] Extreme

Tendency to make extreme, rather than middle, ratings on a rating scale.

[6] Gambling / Cautiousness

Tendency to guess — or not guess — when in doubt.

[7] Overly Positive

Tendency to claim extreme virtue through self-presentation in a superlative manner.

Key Takeaways — Lessons 9 & 10

  • Standardized tests serve screening, diagnostic, and progress-tracking purposes — a pro-testing moderate view.
  • SLD affects psychological processes for using/understanding language; RtI is a multilevel framework to maximize achievement and identify SLDs.
  • RtI has 5 steps and 3 levels (Universal → Small Group → Individual).
  • Dynamic Assessment uses a test → intervention → retest model to explore learning potential.
  • Achievement tests measure degree of learning from a defined experience; aptitude (prognostic) tests predict future performance.
  • WIAT-III covers ages 4–50 with 16 subtests; STEP Battery is used to identify gifted children.
  • Diagnostic tests identify specific deficits for intervention but do not explain the cause.
  • WRMT-III covers ages 4.5–80 with 5 original + 3 new subtests; KeyMath3-DA covers ages 4½–21.
  • K-ABC/KABC-II grounds assessment in Luria's theory (sequential vs. simultaneous) and CHC theory.
  • WJ IV has three co-normed batteries (Achievement, Cognitive Ability, Oral Language) for ages 2–90.
  • Performance, portfolio, and authentic assessment go beyond multiple-choice to evaluate real-world skills.
  • SSHA (Survey of Study Habits & Attitudes) covers 6 areas and yields study skills, attitude, and total orientation scores.
  • Personality = unique, relatively stable constellation of psychological traits.
  • Traits are enduring and distinguishable; types are clustered trait patterns; states are temporary expressions of traits.
  • STAI (Spielberger) distinguishes between trait anxiety (enduring) and state anxiety (temporary).
  • Response styles (acquiescence, halo effect, socially desirable responding) can bias personality assessment results.
