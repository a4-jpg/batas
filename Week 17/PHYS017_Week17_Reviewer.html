01

Work

Definition

What is Work?

In physics, work is done when a force causes an object to move through a displacement. Holding a heavy object without moving it — or pushing a wall that doesn't budge — involves zero mechanical work.

Work Formula

W = F · d · cos θ
W = work (Joules, J)
F = force (Newtons, N)
d = displacement (meters, m)
θ = angle between force & displacement
Types of Work

Positive, Negative & Zero Work

Positive θ = 0°

Force and displacement in the same direction.
e.g., pushing a crate forward

Negative θ = 180°

Force opposes motion.
e.g., friction, braking

Zero θ = 90°

Force perpendicular to displacement.
e.g., carrying a backpack horizontally

02

Energy

Definition

What is Energy?

Energy is the ability to do work. Without energy, no physical process can occur. It comes in many forms — chemical (food, gasoline), electrical (batteries), radiant (sunlight), and mechanical energy.

Kinetic Energy — energy due to motion

KE = ½mv²
m = mass (kg)
v = velocity (m/s)

Gravitational Potential Energy — energy due to position

PE = mgh
m = mass (kg)
g = 9.8 m/s²
h = height (m)
03

Conservation of Energy

Law

Law of Conservation of Energy

Energy cannot be created or destroyed. It can only be transformed from one form to another. Mechanical Energy is the sum of kinetic and potential energy:

ME = KE + PE

Work-Energy Theorem

Wnet = ΔKE

The net work done on an object equals the change in its kinetic energy. More work → greater change in KE.

Special Topic

Escape Velocity

The minimum speed needed to leave a planet permanently without further propulsion.

  • Earth's escape velocity: 11.2 km/s (11,200 m/s)
  • Essential for space exploration, satellite deployment, and rocket engineering
  • Without reaching escape velocity, a spacecraft will fall back to Earth
04

Power

Definition

What is Power?

Power measures how fast work is done. Two students climbing the same stairs do equal work — but the faster student develops more power.

Power Formula

P = W / t
P = power (Watts, W)
W = work (Joules, J)
t = time (seconds, s)

SI unit of power is the Watt: 1 W = 1 J/s

05

Momentum

Definition

Linear Momentum

Momentum describes the quantity of motion possessed by an object. A slow-moving truck can have more momentum than a fast bicycle because of its greater mass.

Momentum Formula

p = mv
p = momentum (kg·m/s)
m = mass (kg)
v = velocity (m/s)
Comparison

Momentum vs. Kinetic Energy

Quantity Formula Effect of doubling speed
Momentum p = mv Doubles (×2)
Kinetic Energy KE = ½mv² Quadruples (×4)

Conservation of Momentum

pi = pf

When no significant external force acts on a system, total momentum before equals total momentum after. e.g., two skaters pushing apart — their momenta are equal and opposite, total stays zero.

Rotational

Angular Momentum

The rotational counterpart of linear momentum. Objects rotating around an axis possess angular momentum — and it is conserved in rotational motion (no external torque).

  • Figure skaters pulling arms in to spin faster
  • Ceiling fans and wind turbines
  • Planets orbiting the Sun
06

Collisions

Types

Types of Collisions

Elastic

Momentum conserved
Kinetic energy conserved
e.g., billiard balls, atomic collisions

Inelastic

Momentum conserved
KE not conserved
e.g., car crashes, clay sticking together

Perfectly Inelastic

Momentum conserved
KE lost to heat/sound/deformation
Objects stick together after collision
07

Summary

Key Takeaways

  • Work transfers energy from one object or system to another
  • Energy cannot be created or destroyed — only transformed
  • Mechanical energy = Kinetic Energy + Potential Energy
  • The Work-Energy Theorem: Wnet = ΔKE
  • Power measures the rate of doing work (P = W/t)
  • Momentum (p = mv) measures quantity of motion
  • Momentum is conserved when no external force acts
  • Elastic collisions conserve both momentum and KE
  • Inelastic collisions conserve only momentum
  • Angular momentum is conserved in rotational motion
