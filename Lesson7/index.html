Overview Mental Health Profession
Definition A multidisciplinary group of professionals that addresses mental health needs in the country. Includes psychiatrists, psychologists, social workers, and others.
  • Identified with professional organizations that regulate training, credentialing, and licensing
  • Adhere to ethical codes and standards of practice
  • Must meet eligibility requirements and competency for their position
👩‍⚕️
Mental Health Professionals The 6 Key Roles
01

Psychiatrists

Medicine

Psychiatry: Branch of medicine focused on the diagnosis, treatment, and prevention of mental, emotional, and behavioral disorders.

Psychiatrists are medical doctors who specialize in mental health — qualified to evaluate both the mental and physical aspects of psychological problems.

02

Psychologists

Behavioral Science

Psychology: The scientific study of human behavior and mental processes.

Psychologists try to understand why humans act the way they do and help disturbed, troubled, and mentally ill individuals.

03

Social Workers

Social Work

Social Work: A practice-based profession that promotes social change, social cohesion, and the empowerment of people.

Help people overcome poverty, discrimination, abuse, addiction, illness, divorce, unemployment, disability, and mental illness.

04

Guidance Counselors

Education

Help students throughout their stay in a learning institution. Work involves assessment, consultation, counseling, research, teaching, and services for students' intellectual, emotional, psychological, social, and behavioral development.

05

Nurses

Healthcare

Nursing: Autonomous and collaborative care of individuals of all ages, families, groups, and communities — sick or well, in all settings.

Includes health promotion, illness prevention, and care of ill, disabled, and dying people.

06

Teachers

Education / Community

Licensed professionals whose technical and professional experience can be used in the community — especially in the barangay — to provide leadership and deliver psychoeducation and psychological first aid.

🤝
Non-Licensed but Trained Mental Health Service Workers & Paraprofessionals

MH Service Workers

Per the Philippine Mental Health Law: trained persons, volunteers, or advocates engaged in mental health promotion, providing support services under the supervision of MH professionals. Can teach skills to patients in non-hospital settings.

Paraprofessionals

Not licensed to practice

Persons who are not licensed to practice but are trained and tasked to assist mental health professionals.

Examples: teacher assistants, aides, volunteers

Therapeutic Personnel

Occupational, recreational, and art therapists who conduct diagnostic tests to determine patients' health status, functional limitations, and the nature of physical/mental disorders.

Nursing Associates

Also: Nurse's Aides

Provide basic nursing and personal care for those in need due to aging, illness, injury, or impairment. Work under the supervision of other health professionals.

Community Health Workers

Also: Barangay Health Workers

Persons who have undergone training under accredited government and NGO programs and voluntarily render primary health care services in the community.

American Red Cross, 2012 Aspects of DMH (Disaster Mental Health) Services
Key Context Mental health professionals and workers serve a wide range — from children to older adults — at different levels and locations. In DMH, work can be tremendous and challenging.
Aspect What it means in a DMH Setting
Worksite DMH work usually takes place in service delivery locations such as shelters, feeding sites, and emergency aid situations — not a typical office.
Teamwork DMH workers may deliver services as part of an outreach team or integrated care team alongside other responders.
Initiation The DMH worker usually initiates contact with clients — unlike regular therapy where clients reach out first.
Relationship Relationships are professional although formal parameters may not always be apparent in a disaster setting.
Duration Interventions are SHORT-TERM; services are often delivered in one face-to-face contact, generally no more than three contacts.
Methodology DMH workers are more directive with clients in disaster situations, focusing on interventions that lead to disaster recovery.
Nature of Interventions Work is task-centered — focused first on meeting clients' urgent needs, rather than a primary focus on emotional states.
Scheduling Interventions are more likely spontaneous and involve outreach efforts in neighborhoods and communities.
Accessibility Clients and families can be difficult to track as housing situations change in the aftermath of a disaster.
Responsibility DMH workers supplement and provide secondary assistance to disaster-affected clients — not the primary responders.
⚠️
Staff Wellbeing Risk Stress, Symptoms & Challenges
The Core Problem The significant amount of work in DMH — including promotion, prevention, treatment, and aftercare — can contribute to HIGH LEVELS OF STRESS in service providers themselves.
Additional Challenges Disaster workers are at risk for compassion fatigue, burnout, and vicarious traumatization. Many find their return to regular duties to be a complicated, prolonged, and difficult process after a disaster response assignment.

Physical Symptoms

Unusual fatigue Sleeping problems Frequent colds Hypertension Cardiovascular disease Diabetes Cancer

Psychological Symptoms

Disorientation Depression Anxiety Substance abuse Violence Suicidal behavior
Important Note Mental health professionals are shown to be reliable responders, but they may experience a decline in well-being and become more vulnerable to mental health problems themselves. They need MORE support for these symptoms.
🛡️
Protecting DMH Workers Solutions & Psychological First Aid
Stress Management Programs DMH workers must also be protected. Stress management programs are good to promote their mental health. This includes: healthy diet · routine exercise · adequate rest
Key Protective Tool

Psychological First Aid (PFA)

A basic understanding of PFA protects both DMH workers and the victims of disaster. It offers first-line psychosocial support after an emergency, disaster, or traumatic event — acting as a foundation for all disaster mental health work.
