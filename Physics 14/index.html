Newton's Laws of Motion

Background

Who is Isaac Newton?

Sir Isaac Newton developed his theories of gravitation in 1666 at age 23. In 1686, he presented his three laws of motion in the Principia Mathematica Philosophiae Naturalis. Together with Kepler's Laws, Newton's work explained why planets move in elliptical orbits rather than circles.

First Law

Law of Inertia

An object at rest remains at rest, and an object in motion remains in motion at constant speed in a straight line unless acted on by an unbalanced force.

Inertia
Second Law

Law of Force

The acceleration of an object depends on the mass of the object and the amount of force applied. A heavier object experiences less acceleration for equal forces.

F = ma
Third Law

Action & Reaction

Whenever one object exerts a force on a second object, the second object exerts an equal and opposite force on the first. Forces result from interactions.

Action–Reaction

Newton's Second Law — Key Formula

Fnet = ma
F = net force (Newtons, N)
m = mass (kg)
a = acceleration (m/s²)

⚠ Valid only for objects with constant mass. Acceleration is proportional to force and inversely proportional to mass.

Real-World Examples

Newton's Laws in Action (Aerodynamics)

LawExample
1st — InertiaA ball falling through the atmosphere, a kite reacting to wind changes
2nd — ForceAircraft motion from aerodynamic forces, weight, and thrust
3rd — Action/ReactionWing lift (air deflected down → wing pushed up); jet engine thrust (gases backward → thrust forward)
02

Static & Kinetic Friction

Definition

What is Frictional Force?

When an object is in contact with a surface, the component of the contact force parallel to the surface is called the frictional force. Friction magnitude does not depend on contact area — only on the normal force and the surface materials.

Static Friction (fs)

Opposes the impending relative motion between two objects that are not yet sliding.

fs ≤ μsF⊥

Can range from zero up to a maximum value fsMax = μsF⊥. Once exceeded, sliding begins.

0 < μs < 1

Kinetic Friction (fk)

Opposes the relative sliding motion that is actually occurring between surfaces.

fk = μkF⊥

Has a fixed value once sliding starts. Generally, μk < μs — kinetic friction is less than maximum static friction.

0 < μk < 1
Key Notes

Important Rules for Friction

  • Friction equations give magnitudes only — direction must be determined from the physics
  • With no other vertical forces: F⊥ = W = mg (the normal force equals weight)
  • Friction force is horizontal; normal force is vertical
  • A sled slows down because kinetic friction opposes its motion

Free-Body Diagrams

Definition

What is an FBD?

A Free-Body Diagram (FBD) shows only the external forces acting on a single object, represented as a point (center of mass) in empty space. Internal forces are ignored because only external forces affect an object's motion. FBDs are the essential first step in applying Newton's Laws.

Procedure

7-Step FBD Method

01

Draw a center-of-mass symbol (⊗) to represent the object. If multiple objects are involved, draw a separate FBD for each.

02

List all items in contact with the object. These exert contact forces. Do NOT add these items to your drawing — only their forces.

03

Identify all forces exerted on the object: (a) gravitational force from Earth and (b) contact forces from each item in Step 2.

04

Draw an arrow for each force, pointing in the direction the force acts, with the tail at the center of mass. Length should reflect relative magnitude. Label as Ftypeby/on.

05

Verify all forces are exerted on (not by) the object. Every label should share the same second subscript letter.

06

Draw the acceleration vector next to the center of mass. If object is in equilibrium, write ā = 0 and ensure forces sum to zero.

07

Draw a reference axis. If accelerating, point +x in the direction of acceleration. Final diagram: only the ⊗, force arrows, axis, and acceleration.

FBD Example

Book Lying Motionless on the Floor

Two forces act on the book: gravity (Earth pulls down) and normal force (floor pushes up). Since the book is at rest, ā = 0 and these forces are equal and opposite.

F͢ᶜfb = contact force by floor on book (↑)  |  F͢ᴳEb = gravity by Earth on book (↓)

Tension Force

Definition

What is Tension?

Tension is a force along the length of a medium — particularly a flexible medium like a rope or cable (from Latin tendere: "to stretch"). Cables and ropes transmit forces through tension.

Key Tension Rules

T = FHand

The tension magnitude equals the applied hand force. A massless rope transmits tension undiminished from one end to the other. A massless, frictionless pulley also transmits tension undiminished.

If a mass is at rest or moving at constant velocity:  ΣF = W + T = 0 → T = mg
Newton's 3rd Law Pairs

Tension Action–Reaction Pairs

ObjectForce PairDescription
Box end(F̄_Box, T̄)Tension pulls on box; box pulls on rope
Hand end(F̄_Hand, −T̄)Tension pulls on hand; hand pulls on rope

⚠ The weight of the person holding must be greater than the weight of the load, or the load drops and tension pulls the person upward.

Problem-Solving Strategy

4-Step Approach

STEP 1 Identify Newton's Laws as the relevant principle. Draw a detailed sketch with labeled force arrows.
STEP 2 List knowns and unknowns. Identify the system of interest. Distinguish external vs internal forces.
STEP 3 Apply F_net = ma. For 2D problems, break into x and y components: F_net,x = ma; F_net,y = 0 (if no vertical acceleration).
STEP 4 Check: Is the answer reasonable? Do the units match? (If solving for force, you should get N — not m/s.)

Worked Examples

Example 1

Rocket Acceleration — find a

▼

Given

  • m = 5.00 × 10⁵ kg
  • Thrust (Ft) = 1.250 × 10⁷ N
  • Air resistance (Fair) = 4.50 × 10⁶ N
  • System: rocket accelerating straight up

Set Up Net Force

Forces acting: Thrust ↑, Weight ↓ (mg), Air resistance ↓

F_net = F_t − F_air − mg = ma

Solve for Acceleration

a = (F_t − F_air − mg) / m

Plug in Values

a = (1.250×10⁷ − 4.50×10⁶ − (5.00×10⁵)(9.80)) / (5.00×10⁵)

Answer

a = 6.20 m/s²

Example 2

High Jumper — find force exerted

▼

Given

  • m = 70.0 kg
  • a = 4.00 × g = 4.00 × 9.80 = 39.2 m/s²
  • System: jumper accelerating upward

Set Up Net Force

Forces: Ground reaction F ↑, Weight W = mg ↓

F − mg = ma

Solve for F

F = ma + mg = m(a + g)

Plug in Values

F = (70.0)(39.2) + (70.0)(9.80) = 2744 + 686

Answer

F = 3.43 × 10³ N

Example 3

Freight Train — find force per engine

▼

Given

  • 2 engines × 8.00×10⁴ kg = 1.60×10⁵ kg
  • 45 cars × 5.50×10⁴ kg = 2.475×10⁶ kg
  • Total mass = 2.635×10⁶ kg
  • a = 5.00×10⁻² m/s²
  • Friction (f) = 7.50×10⁵ N

Set Up Net Force

F − f = ma

Solve for Total Force F

F = ma + f

F = (2.635×10⁶)(5.00×10⁻²) + 7.50×10⁵ = 8.8175×10⁵ N

Force Per Engine (2 identical engines)

F_one = 8.8175×10⁵ / 2

Answer

F per engine = 4.41 × 10⁵ N

Example 4

Car & Boat Trailer — find friction

▼

Given

  • m (car) = 1100 kg
  • m (boat + trailer) = 700 kg
  • Total mass = 1800 kg
  • Driving force = 1900 N
  • a = 0.550 m/s²

Set Up Net Force

F_drive − f = ma

Solve for Friction f

f = F_drive − ma

f = 1900 − (1800)(0.550) = 1900 − 990

Answer

f = 910 N

Summary

Key Takeaways

  • Newton's 1st Law: inertia — objects resist changes in motion
  • Newton's 2nd Law: F_net = ma (constant mass only)
  • Newton's 3rd Law: every action has an equal, opposite reaction
  • Static friction opposes impending motion; f_s ≤ μ_s × F_⊥
  • Kinetic friction opposes actual sliding; f_k = μ_k × F_⊥
  • Friction magnitude does NOT depend on contact area
  • FBDs show only external forces acting ON the system
  • A massless rope transmits tension magnitude undiminished
  • At equilibrium (a = 0): net force in every direction = 0
  • Always check units — force answers should be in Newtons (N)
