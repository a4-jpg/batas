LESSON 7

Test Development Process

From idea to standardized instrument — the full cycle

Overview

What is Test Development?

Test development is an umbrella term for everything that goes into the process of creating a test — conception, construction, tryout, analysis, and revision. It unfolds in five sequential stages.

The 5 Stages

Stages of Test Development

STAGE 01
Test Conceptualization

Conceiving ideas for the test — identifying the need, purpose, and target population.

STAGE 02
Test Construction

Writing items, formatting, setting scoring rules, and building the test structure.

STAGE 03
Test Tryout

Administering the test to a representative sample under conditions matching the final version.

STAGE 04
Item Analysis

Statistical evaluation of items — which are good, need revision, or should be discarded.

STAGE 05
Test Revision

Modifying content or format to improve the test's effectiveness as a measurement tool.

01

Test Conceptualization

Concept

Where Tests Begin

The origins of any published test can be traced to self-talk: "There ought to be a test designed to measure [X] in [such and such] a way." Common triggers include:

  • Existing tests that lack psychometric soundness
  • Emerging social phenomena or patterns of behavior (e.g., LGBTQ+ experiences, new mental disorders)
  • A need to assess mastery in a new occupation or profession (e.g., data analytics)
Preliminary Questions

Questions to Ask Before Building

  • What is the test designed to measure?
  • What is the objective of the test?
  • Is there a need for this test?
  • Who will use this test? Who will take it?
  • What content will the test cover?
  • How will the test be administered?
  • What is the ideal format of the test?
  • Should more than one form of the test be developed?
  • What special training will be required of test users?
  • What types of responses will be required of test takers?
  • Who benefits from an administration of this test?
  • Is there any potential for harm from this test?
  • How will meaning be attributed to scores on this test?
Pilot Work / Pilot Study

Preliminary research surrounding the creation of a test prototype. Includes literature reviews, experimentation, and the creation, revision, and deletion of preliminary test items to determine how best to measure the targeted construct.

Key Distinction

Norm-Referenced vs. Criterion-Referenced Tests

Type What it measures Typical use Item goal
Norm-Referenced Performance relative to a group; high scorers answer correctly, low scorers do not IQ tests, standardized aptitude tests Maximize spread among test takers
Criterion-Referenced Whether test taker has met specific criteria/mastery Licensing exams, educational mastery tests Discriminate between those who have and haven't mastered the skill

For criterion-referenced tests, development typically involves two groups: one that has mastered the skill and one that has not. Items that best discriminate between them are considered "good" items.

02

Test Construction

A

Scaling

Scaling

The process of setting rules for assigning numbers in measurement — designing and calibrating a measuring device and assigning values to different amounts of the trait being measured. L.L. Thurstone is credited for pioneering methodologically sound scaling methods and introducing absolute scaling (measuring item difficulty across samples of varying ability).

Types of Scales

Scale Types

[1] AGE-BASED
Age-Based Scale

Used when test performance as a function of age is the critical interest.

[2] GRADE-BASED
Grade-Based Scale

Used when test performance as a function of grade is the critical interest.

[3] STANINE
Stanine Scale

All raw scores are transformed into a 1–9 range.

[4] UNIDIMENSIONAL
Unidimensional Scale

Only one dimension is presumed to underlie the ratings.

[5] MULTIDIMENSIONAL
Multidimensional Scale

More than one dimension is thought to guide test taker responses.

Scaling Methods

Methods of Scaling

# Method Data Level Key Notes
[1] Rating Scale Ordinal Words/statements used to rate strength of trait. Can be unidimensional or multidimensional. Includes Likert Scale (5- or 7-point; weights 1–5; negative items reverse scored).
[2] Method of Paired Comparison Ordinal Test takers compare pairs of stimuli; score reflects how often choices agree with judges' ratings. Forces a choice between items.
[3] Sorting Task Ordinal Stimuli sorted using Comparative Scale (rank vs. every other item) or Categorical Scale (placed into categories on a continuum).
[4] Guttman Scale Ordinal Items range from weaker to stronger expressions. Agreeing with stronger statements implies agreeing with milder ones. Uses Scalogram Analysis.
[5] Method of Equal-Appearing Intervals Interval (presumed) Introduced by Thurstone. Respondents select statements reflecting their attitudes; values are averaged to produce a score.
B

Writing Items

Item Pool

The reservoir of items from which final test items will (or will not) be drawn. For a standardized multiple-choice test, the first draft should have approximately twice the number of items needed for the final version. The pool should be multiplied by the number of alternate forms planned.

Item Formats

Selected-Response vs. Constructed-Response

Format What Test Takers Do Subtypes
Selected-Response Choose the correct answer from given alternatives Multiple Choice — stem + correct option + distractors/foils
Matching Items — premises (left) matched to responses (right); lists should be short & homogeneous
Binary Choice (True-False) — only two options; 50% guessing probability
Constructed-Response Supply or create the answer Completion Item — fill in a word/phrase; must have a specific correct answer
Short-Answer Item — brief response; no strict length rule
Essay Item — demonstrates depth of knowledge; limited breadth, subject to scorer subjectivity
Computer Administration

Digital Testing Concepts

TERM
Item Bank

A large, accessible collection of test items classified by subject, statistics, or other variables. Items can be added, withdrawn, or modified.

TERM
Item Branching

The computer tailors content and order of items based on responses to previous items — the core mechanism of adaptive testing.

TERM
Computerized Adaptive Testing (CAT)

Items presented are based on prior performance. Can reduce items administered by up to 50% while simultaneously reducing measurement error by 50%. Also reduces floor and ceiling effects.

Scoring Models

How Test Scores Are Assigned

MODEL 1
Cumulative Model

Higher score = higher level of the trait. The most commonly used model due to its simplicity and logic.

MODEL 2
Class / Category Scoring

Responses earn credit toward placement in a diagnostic category. Used when a certain number of symptoms must be exhibited.

MODEL 3
Ipsative Scoring

Compares one scale within a test to another scale within the same test. Used for intra-individual comparisons only — not appropriate for inter-individual comparisons.

03

Test Tryout

Stage 3

Conducting the Test Tryout

  • Administer to people similar in critical respects to the intended test population.
  • Use no fewer than 5 subjects, and preferably 10, per item on the test — the more the better.
  • Too few subjects risks phantom factors — artifacts of small sample size that emerge in factor analysis.
  • Conditions should be as identical as possible to conditions under which the standardized version will be administered.
04

Item Analysis

Item Analysis

A general term for a set of methods used to evaluate test items — one of the most important aspects of test development. Methods are mostly quantitative (statistical) but qualitative procedures also exist.

Four Core Indices

What Gets Analyzed

Item Difficulty Index
p

Proportion of test takers who answered correctly. Optimal average is ~.50, with individual items ranging from .30 to .80. Called item-endorsement index in personality testing.

Item Reliability Index
s × r

Internal consistency indicator. Equal to the product of the item-score standard deviation (s) and the correlation (r) between item score and total test score. Higher = more internally consistent.

Item Validity Index
validity

Indicates degree to which a test measures what it purports to measure. Higher = greater criterion-related validity. Important when maximizing criterion-related validity is the goal.

Item Discrimination Index
d

Measures how well an item separates high scorers from low scorers. Optimal boundary: upper and lower 27% of the distribution. A negative d = bad item (low scorers answer correctly more often → revise or delete).

Factor Analysis (in Item Analysis)

A statistical tool to determine if items appear to be measuring the same construct. Items that don't "load on" their intended factor can be revised or eliminated. Also useful for improving inter-item consistency and comparing response patterns across groups.

Other Considerations

Guessing, Fairness & Speed Tests

IssueKey Points
Guessing Corrections for guessing (formula scores) must account for: (1) guessing is not random, (2) omitted items, and (3) lucky vs. unlucky guessers. No fully satisfactory solution exists. Test manuals should include explicit instructions on guessing and how to score omitted items.
Item Fairness A biased item favors one group over another when group ability is controlled. Item-characteristic curves (ICCs) are used to detect bias — if ICCs differ significantly across groups, the item is biased.
Speed Tests Items near the end may appear harder simply because test takers run out of time. Test developers should administer the tryout with generous time limits to get accurate item analysis data.
Qualitative Methods

Qualitative Item Analysis

Non-statistical procedures that explore how individual items work through verbal means (interviews, group discussions). Two key approaches:

METHOD 1
"Think Aloud" Test Administration

Respondents verbalize thoughts as they answer. Reveals not just if an item is misinterpreted but why and how — especially useful for diagnosing low vs. high scorer errors.

METHOD 2
Expert Panels & Sensitivity Reviews

Experts examine items for fairness, offensive language, and stereotypes. Also helps develop tools for underserved populations and tailor items to specific cultural groups.

05

Test Revision

Revision Process

Steps in Test Revision

  1. Characterize each item by its strengths and weaknesses (reliability, validity, bias, difficulty).
  2. Balance strengths and weaknesses across items, keeping the test's purpose in mind.
  3. Eliminate poor items from the pool; retain items shown to be good in the tryout.
  4. Administer the revised test to a second appropriate sample under standardized conditions.
  5. Develop norms from this second sample → the test is now "standardized."
  6. If still unsatisfactory, repeat revision → tryout → item analysis until the test is acceptable.
  7. Await cross-validation before drawing final conclusions about the test's validity.
When to Revise an Existing Test

Conditions That Trigger Revision

  • Stimulus materials look dated and current test takers can't relate
  • Verbal content contains outdated vocabulary no longer understood by current takers
  • Words/phrases have taken on new or offensive meanings in popular culture
  • Norms are inadequate due to changes in the population of potential test takers
  • Age-related shifts require extending norms upward or downward
  • Reliability or validity can be significantly improved by revision
  • The underlying theory has been significantly updated
Key Terms in Revision

Cross-Validation & Related Concepts

TERM
Cross-Validation

Revalidating a test on a different sample from the one used to originally establish validity. Mandatory step for all new and revised tests.

TERM
Validity Shrinkage

The expected decrease in item validities that inevitably occurs after cross-validation. Considered a normal part of test development.

TERM
Co-Validation / Co-Norming

Validating (or norming) two or more tests simultaneously using the same sample of test takers.

IRT Applications

Item Response Theory in Test Building & Revision

ApplicationWhat it Does
[1] Evaluating existing tests IRT information curves help evaluate how well individual items (or entire tests) measure different levels of the construct; used to weed out uninformative or redundant items.
[2] Differential Item Functioning (DIF) DIF occurs when an item functions differently for two groups with the same underlying trait level. DIF analysis scrutinizes item response curves group-by-group; DIF items may be biased.
[3] Developing item banks Items are evaluated qualitatively and quantitatively, administered to a large representative sample, then assessed for validity, reliability, domain coverage, and DIF. The final item bank measures a single domain and can be used to generate multiple fixed tests or CAT.

Key Takeaways — Lesson 7

  • Test development has 5 stages: Conceptualization → Construction → Tryout → Item Analysis → Revision
  • Norm-referenced tests compare test takers to each other; criterion-referenced tests assess mastery of specific criteria
  • Scaling assigns numbers to different amounts of a trait; major methods include Rating Scales, Paired Comparison, Sorting, Guttman, and Equal-Appearing Intervals
  • Likert Scale is a summative rating scale with 5–7 options; negatively worded items are reverse scored
  • Item pool should be roughly double the final test size; first draft ≈ 2× needed items
  • CAT reduces items administered and measurement error by up to 50%
  • The three scoring models are Cumulative, Class/Category, and Ipsative
  • Item analysis evaluates difficulty (p), reliability (s×r), validity, and discrimination (d)
  • Optimal item difficulty is ~.50; individual items should range from .30 to .80
  • The discrimination index (d) compares upper vs. lower 27% of the score distribution; negative d = bad item
  • Cross-validation is mandatory; validity shrinkage is expected and normal
  • DIF (Differential Item Functioning) detects items that are biased against specific groups
