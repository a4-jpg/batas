Fundamentals of morality and moral dilemmas

What makes something a moral matter, and what to do when duties collide.

Ethics
Systematic study of right and wrong.
Morality
Practical application of ethical principles in conduct.
Moral dilemma
Conflicting obligations; fulfilling one means violating another.
Justice
Fair and equitable treatment of people.
Social construct
Idea created and maintained by society.
Universal truth / justice
Valid across cultures; impartial, equal treatment.

Memory hookMoral standards ask “Right or wrong?” Non-moral standards ask “Appropriate or inappropriate?”

Biblical angle: Micah 6:8 (justice, mercy, humility) and Proverbs 21:3 (doing right beats ritual). Customs never replace justice, honesty, and love.

Moral dilemmas

Three conditions: an obligation to act exists; several conflicting actions are open; each has strong arguments. Example: a drunk-driving friend asks for a false alibi (loyalty vs. honesty and justice).

Individual

Personal conscience. A found wallet your family needs for medical bills.

Organizational

Personal ethics vs. company policy. A firm secretly sells unsafe products.

Structural

Social systems. Economic survival vs. public health.

Four foundations of moral reasoning

Freedom→Responsibility→Reason→Impartiality→Ethical decision
  • Freedom: no praise or blame without meaningful autonomy.
  • Responsibility: free choices make us accountable for effects on others.
  • Reason: judgments need evidence and logic, not emotion alone.
  • Impartiality: everyone’s welfare counts equally; no automatic favoritism.
TakeawayMoral reasoning is not deciding what we prefer. It means identifying standards, recognizing conflicting obligations, and judging responsibly.

Culture, relativism, and Filipino values

Culture shapes how we understand morality, but culture alone cannot determine it.

Cultural relativism

Beliefs and practices should be understood in their cultural context. Encourages understanding before judging.

Ethnocentrism

Treating our own culture as the standard for judging others.

Cultural difference ≠ moral differenceChopsticks vs. fork is cultural. Deception, harm, discrimination, and dignity raise real ethical questions.

Why relativism alone fails

  • If acceptance made things right, harmful practices (discrimination, exploitation, corruption, denial of rights) could not be criticized.
  • “Everyone cheats here” does not make cheating right. Widely accepted can still be wrong.
  • People do criticize their own cultures, so culture is not the final authority.
  • Understanding ≠ approving. Knowing why utang na loob matters does not justify every demand made in its name.

Asian moral emphasis (broad tendencies, not universal rules)

Relationships, family, social harmony, duties tied to roles (child, parent, student, teacher, leader, citizen). Ask “What is best for me and the people connected to me?” Aim for balance between individual dignity and relational responsibility. Harmony becomes a problem when it means silence about wrongdoing, such as not reporting plagiarism to avoid conflict.

Practice: dilemmas to analyze

Arts career vs. parents’ wishes. Friends say “friends help friends” and want you to cheat. A relative asks a favor for someone who once helped your family. A bullied student stays silent to spare family embarrassment. A community leader abuses power but exposing him creates conflict. For each: What value? Why does it matter? What is the ethical problem? Would relativism settle it? What would you do, and can you defend it?

Virtue ethics: Aristotle and Aquinas

Not only “What should I do?” but “What kind of person am I becoming?”

Choices→Actions→Habits→Character→Virtue→Purpose (telos)→Flourishing
Virtue
Good quality of character that helps us consistently choose right.
Character
Who you have become through attitudes, choices, habits.
Telos
End, goal, purpose: “What is this for?”
Eudaimonia
Aristotle’s flourishing: living well, not a passing feeling.
Phronesis
Practical wisdom: judging what to do in a real situation.
Beatitude
Aquinas: perfect happiness, fulfilled in God.
Moral disposition
Developed tendency to respond to moral situations in a certain way.

Key ideas

  • Habit: we become what we repeatedly practice. One honest act is good; repeated honesty becomes character. Cheating works the same way in reverse.
  • Means vs. ultimate goal: studying → passing → graduating → job → good life. Aristotle asks for the goal pursued for its own sake: eudaimonia.
  • Happiness is more than feeling. A rich, dishonest person is not flourishing; an honest, caring one may be.
  • Golden Mean: the fitting response between extremes, not mere halfway. Cowardice ← Courage → Recklessness. Too little anger ← appropriate → rage.
  • Practical wisdom example: a friend asks about a weak presentation. “Perfect!” is kind but false; “Terrible” is honest but cold; wisdom combines both with an offer to help.

Aristotle vs. Aquinas

AristotleAquinas (1225–1274)
Ultimate goal is eudaimoniaUltimate end is God
Virtue is necessary for flourishingVirtue is necessary for moral and spiritual development
Happiness means living wellComplete happiness cannot come from created things alone
Reason guides moral actionReason matters within a Christian framework

Aquinas’s virtues: justice (giving each their due), courage (facing difficulty for the good), temperance (desires do not rule us), prudence (wise decisions).

Three questionsWhat kind of person am I becoming? What kind of life am I trying to live? What is my ultimate purpose?
Application: the future teacher

A close friend and fellow teacher changes grades and says, “Don’t report me.” Virtue ethics asks what action to take and what habit silence would build. Virtues at stake: justice, honesty, courage, compassion, practical wisdom.

Natural Law, Kant, and right theories

Different theories can agree on an action while disagreeing about why it is right.

Natural Law (Aquinas)

Objective right and wrong grounded in human nature and practical reason. First principle: good is to be done and pursued, evil avoided (Summa Theologiae I–II, q. 94, a. 2).

Eternal law→Natural law→Human law
  • Basic goods: life, family, knowledge, social relationships, reasonable conduct.
  • Legal ≠ moral: a government cannot make intentional killing of the innocent good.
  • Strengths: objective standard, criticizes unjust laws, ties ethics to flourishing.
  • Challenges: what does human nature really require? Cultures and thinkers disagree on which goods are fundamental.

Kant (1724–1804), deontology

Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals (1785). Rightness is not mainly about consequences.

Good will
Good in itself. Intelligence, wealth, power can be misused. Not the same as “good intentions”: what counts is acting from duty.
Duty
Moral requirement known by reason: “I ought,” not “I want.” Doing right because it is right.
Hypothetical imperative
If you want X, do Y. Depends on a goal.
Categorical imperative
Do this because it is morally required, whatever you want.
Formula of universal lawAct only on a maxim you can at the same time will to be a universal law.
Identify action→Find maxim (why?)→Universalize→Consistent?

Lying to escape trouble fails: if everyone did it, trust and communication collapse. Humanity formula: treat persons always as ends, never merely as means. Paying a teacher is fine; lying to a worker about a promotion just to push productivity is not. Kant is not “follow any rule”: unjust laws or unethical orders are not automatically right.

Natural Law puts the good before the right; standard Kantian deontology grounds rightness in principle. Virtue ethics: right is what a virtuous person would characteristically do.

Case: the lost phone

All three theories say return it. Natural Law weighs property, honesty, trust. Kant tests the maxim “I may keep valuables when unseen.” Consequentialism weighs losses and lost trust. Same verdict, different reasons.

Case: cheating to keep a scholarship

Kant: the maxim “cheat whenever it helps” cannot be universalized. Natural Law: undermines truth, knowledge, justice, fairness. Consequentialism: unfair to others, weakens credentials and trust.

Ethical maturityIt is not just knowing the answer but explaining why it is morally justified.

Feelings, reason, and moral courage

Feel, judge, evaluate, decide, act courageously.

Central principleFeelings awaken moral concern; reason evaluates it; impartiality disciplines it; courage puts it into action; character makes it a habit.

1. Feelings and decisions

Feeling: conscious affective state. Emotion: affective, physiological, and cognitive response. Moral motivation: the force from recognizing to acting. Empathy: sharing another’s state. Compassion motivates helping, guilt discourages harm, indignation signals injustice, but emotion can distort when selective, excessive, or cut off from facts. Scholarship case: awarding the crying student solely for emotional display, over an equally needy calm one, is understandable but questionable.

2. Feelings and judgment

Moral intuition
Automatic response before conscious reasoning.
Moral emotion
Guilt, shame, compassion, indignation.
Moral relativism
Truth or justification relative to cultural or individual frameworks.
Partiality
Special consideration for those close to us.

Distinguish “I feel this is wrong” from “I have sufficient reasons to conclude it is wrong.” Identifiable victim: a campaign showing one starving child draws ₱5,000 gifts while thousands of equally needy children go unseen. Emotional visibility does not set moral weight.

3. Reason and impartiality

  • Reason weighs evidence; moral reasoning is practical reasoning about what we ought to do; a principle is a general standard.
  • Impartiality: no unjustified preference for self, friends, or favored groups. Consistency: same principle in comparable cases. Universalizability: standards apply across comparable situations.
  • Impartial test: “Would I weigh the same factors if none of the applicants were my friend?”
Core statement (Hume + Kant)Emotion tells us something matters; reason helps decide how much moral weight it deserves.

4. Moral courage

Courage: virtue of responding well to fear or danger. Moral courage: standing by justified values despite fear, opposition, pressure, or risk; facing social, professional, or personal costs. Physical courage: facing bodily danger. Courage is not absence of fear but not letting fear dictate action. Attributes: integrity, responsibility, honesty, advocacy, perseverance, acceptance of risk. Example: reporting cheating through proper channels despite being called a “snitch.”

5. Developing moral courage

Moral character
Stable pattern of virtues and habits.
Practical wisdom
Discerning what to do in this situation.
Moral resilience
Keeping commitments despite setbacks.
  1. Clarify principles: “What do I believe is right, and why?”
  2. Question emotions: reliable information or bias?
  3. Practice small acts: admit mistakes, refuse to cheat, correct misinformation.
  4. Prepare for pressure: anticipate “What if my friends reject me?”
  5. Act through appropriate channels: reason → judgment → prudence → action, not public humiliation.
Case: the viral donation

A video of a malnourished child goes viral; a student gives most of their savings, then learns other local children are equally affected and the campaign lacks transparency. Ask: What emotions drove the gift? Did the story make this the most deserving case? What further information matters? What if questioning brings criticism? What habits make giving responsible?

TakeawayMoral maturity is not eliminating emotion but integrating emotion, reason, impartiality, and courage in pursuit of the right.
